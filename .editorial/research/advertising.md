# Advertising — Verified Bibliography

Curated for the Advertising chapter. Every entry below has a DOI verified against the Crossref REST API (`https://api.crossref.org/works/<doi>`) on 2026-06-21; titles, authors, year, journal, and pagination were checked against the Crossref version-of-record metadata. Where a preprint (SSRN/NBER) and a published article exist, only the **published version-of-record DOI** is cited.

Scope: top-4 marketing journals (Journal of Marketing [JM], Journal of Marketing Research [JMR], Marketing Science [MktSci], Journal of Consumer Research [JCR]) plus Quantitative Marketing and Economics [QME], Marketing Letters, and a small number of adjacent economics/econometrics venues (QJE, Econometrica, Management Science) where they are the standard references for advertising measurement.

Section tags used: `elasticity`, `how-it-works`, `digital`, `measurement/experiments`, `targeting-privacy`, `content`.

## Empirical Generalizations: Advertising Elasticity

```bibtex
@article{sethuraman2011advertising,
  author  = {Sethuraman, Raj and Tellis, Gerard J. and Briesch, Richard A.},
  title   = {How Well Does Advertising Work? Generalizations from Meta-Analysis of Brand Advertising Elasticities},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {2011},
  volume  = {48},
  number  = {3},
  pages   = {457--471},
  doi     = {10.1509/jmkr.48.3.457}
}
```
Meta-analysis of 751 short-term and 402 long-term brand-advertising elasticity estimates (drawing on 56 studies). The mean short-term advertising elasticity is about 0.12 — roughly a quarter of the comparable price elasticity — and has declined over time; the long-term elasticity is about 0.24. Elasticities are higher early in the life cycle, for durable goods, and in print media. The headline reference for "how small advertising elasticities really are."
**Section:** elasticity

```bibtex
@article{assmus1984advertising,
  author  = {Assmus, Gert and Farley, John U. and Lehmann, Donald R.},
  title   = {How Advertising Affects Sales: Meta-Analysis of Econometric Results},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {1984},
  volume  = {21},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {65--74},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224378402100107}
}
```
The original meta-analysis of econometric advertising-response studies (128 elasticity estimates across 22 studies). It established the modern benchmark — a mean short-run advertising-to-sales elasticity around 0.22 — and showed how model specification (functional form, lag structure, data interval) systematically moves the estimate. The methodological template that Sethuraman, Tellis, and Briesch later extended.
**Section:** elasticity

```bibtex
@article{clarke1976duration,
  author  = {Clarke, Darral G.},
  title   = {Econometric Measurement of the Duration of Advertising Effect on Sales},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {1976},
  volume  = {13},
  number  = {4},
  pages   = {345--357},
  doi     = {10.2307/3151017}
}
```
Reviews 70 econometric models and shows that the apparent "carryover" or duration of advertising's sales effect is largely an artifact of the data interval used (data-interval bias): studies using monthly or quarterly data overstate the duration relative to the true effect, which decays within 3-15 months for frequently purchased products. Foundational for understanding advertising dynamics and temporal aggregation.
**Section:** elasticity, how-it-works

```bibtex
@article{leone1995carryover,
  author  = {Leone, Robert P.},
  title   = {Generalizing What Is Known About Temporal Aggregation and Advertising Carryover},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {1995},
  volume  = {14},
  number  = {3, Supplement},
  pages   = {G141--G150},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.14.3.g141}
}
```
A Marketing Science "empirical generalizations" synthesis that reconciles the literature on advertising carryover and temporal aggregation. Correcting for data-interval bias across studies, Leone generalizes that the duration of the advertising effect on sales is roughly 6-9 months and that the implied 90% duration interval is short. The canonical generalization on advertising carryover.
**Section:** elasticity, how-it-works

## How Advertising Works / Persuasion

```bibtex
@article{vakratsas1999advertising,
  author  = {Vakratsas, Demetrios and Ambler, Tim},
  title   = {How Advertising Works: What Do We Really Know?},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {1999},
  volume  = {63},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {26--43},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224299906300103}
}
```
A review of more than 250 articles and books that organizes advertising-response theory into a taxonomy of intermediate effects (cognition, affect, experience) and the sequencing among them. Concludes there is no single hierarchy-of-effects model that fits all situations and that the cognition-affect-experience triad, plus the absence of a universal ordering, is the most defensible generalization. The standard conceptual map of "how advertising works."
**Section:** how-it-works

```bibtex
@article{tellis1988advertising,
  author  = {Tellis, Gerard J.},
  title   = {Advertising Exposure, Loyalty, and Brand Purchase: A Two-Stage Model of Choice},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {1988},
  volume  = {25},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {134--144},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224378802500202}
}
```
Uses single-source scanner data linking household TV advertising exposure to actual purchases to estimate a two-stage (brand-choice then quantity) logit model. Finds advertising's effect on brand choice is small and works mainly by reinforcing existing preferences of loyal buyers rather than winning new ones, with diminishing returns to exposure. An early, identification-conscious demonstration of advertising's modest behavioral effect using individual-level exposure data.
**Section:** how-it-works, elasticity

## Long-Term and Dynamic Effects

```bibtex
@article{mela1997longterm,
  author  = {Mela, Carl F. and Gupta, Sunil and Lehmann, Donald R.},
  title   = {The Long-Term Impact of Promotion and Advertising on Consumer Brand Choice},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {1997},
  volume  = {34},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {248--261},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224379703400205}
}
```
Uses 8+ years of household scanner panel data with a time-varying choice model to separate long-run from short-run effects. Finds that sustained advertising makes consumers less price- and promotion-sensitive over time, while heavy promotion makes them more deal-prone — long-term effects that are invisible in short-window analyses. A foundational study on advertising's enduring, preference-shaping role.
**Section:** how-it-works, elasticity

```bibtex
@article{dekimpe1995persistence,
  author  = {Dekimpe, Marnik G. and Hanssens, Dominique M.},
  title   = {The Persistence of Marketing Effects on Sales},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {1995},
  volume  = {14},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {1--21},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.14.1.1}
}
```
Introduces persistence modeling (unit-root and vector-autoregression / impulse-response methods) to marketing to estimate the long-run, dynamic impact of advertising and other actions on sales. Distinguishes business-as-usual fluctuations from permanent (persistent) effects, providing the time-series toolkit used to quantify advertising's long-run sales elasticity. Methodologically foundational for the long-run-effects literature.
**Section:** how-it-works, measurement/experiments

## TV Advertising Effectiveness (Single-Source & Split-Cable Experiments)

```bibtex
@article{lodish1995tvadvertising,
  author  = {Lodish, Leonard M. and Abraham, Magid and Kalmenson, Stuart and Livelsberger, Jeanne and Lubetkin, Beth and Richardson, Bruce and Stevens, Mary Ellen},
  title   = {How {T.V.} Advertising Works: A Meta-Analysis of 389 Real World Split Cable {T.V.} Advertising Experiments},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {1995},
  volume  = {32},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {125--139},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224379503200201}
}
```
Meta-analysis of 389 IRI BehaviorScan split-cable field experiments — true randomized controlled tests in which matched households received different TV ad weight. Finds that increasing TV advertising weight does not reliably increase sales for established brands (only about half of weight tests had significant effects), but creative/copy and media-plan changes matter more than sheer weight, and effects are larger for new products. The benchmark field-experimental evidence on TV advertising.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, how-it-works

```bibtex
@article{tirunillai2017offlinetv,
  author  = {Tirunillai, Seshadri and Tellis, Gerard J.},
  title   = {Does Offline {TV} Advertising Affect Online Chatter? Quasi-Experimental Analysis Using Synthetic Control},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {2017},
  volume  = {36},
  number  = {6},
  pages   = {862--878},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.2017.1040}
}
```
Identifies the causal effect of offline TV advertising on online word-of-mouth using a synthetic-control quasi-experiment across many brands. Finds TV advertising causes a small but significant short-lived increase in the volume (not the valence) of online chatter, with effects varying by ad content and timing. A clean identification design bridging traditional TV media and digital response.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

## Digital, Display & Search Advertising — Effectiveness

```bibtex
@article{blake2015paidsearch,
  author  = {Blake, Thomas and Nosko, Chris and Tadelis, Steven},
  title   = {Consumer Heterogeneity and Paid Search Effectiveness: A Large-Scale Field Experiment},
  journal = {Econometrica},
  year    = {2015},
  volume  = {83},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {155--174},
  doi     = {10.3982/ecta12423}
}
```
Large-scale randomized field experiment at eBay turning paid search advertising on and off across markets. Finds returns to brand-keyword search ads are negative (users would have arrived via organic links anyway) and returns to non-brand keywords are small and concentrated among new/infrequent users, so naive observational ROI estimates are massively overstated. A landmark on selection bias in digital advertising measurement.
**Section:** digital, measurement/experiments

```bibtex
@article{simonov2018brandkeywords,
  author  = {Simonov, Andrey and Nosko, Chris and Rao, Justin M.},
  title   = {Competition and Crowd-Out for Brand Keywords in Sponsored Search},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {2018},
  volume  = {37},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {200--215},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.2017.1065}
}
```
Studies bidding on a firm's own brand keywords using a large search-engine field experiment and observational data. Shows that competitor "poaching" of a brand's keyword raises the incremental value of defensive own-brand advertising, reconciling the Blake-Nosko-Tadelis "no incremental value" finding with cases where defending brand terms pays off. Refines when branded search advertising is and is not wasteful.
**Section:** digital, measurement/experiments

```bibtex
@article{sahni2016spillovers,
  author  = {Sahni, Navdeep S.},
  title   = {Advertising Spillovers: Evidence from Online Field Experiments and Implications for Returns on Advertising},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {2016},
  volume  = {53},
  number  = {4},
  pages   = {459--478},
  doi     = {10.1509/jmr.14.0274}
}
```
Uses randomized online advertising field experiments on a restaurant-search platform to show that a focal advertiser's ads also raise sales of competing, non-advertised firms (positive category/spillover effects). Because standard ROI calculations ignore these spillovers, they can misstate the true effect of advertising. Important for category-level vs. brand-level interpretation of digital ad returns.
**Section:** digital, measurement/experiments

```bibtex
@article{sahni2015temporal,
  author  = {Sahni, Navdeep S.},
  title   = {Effect of Temporal Spacing Between Advertising Exposures: Evidence from Online Field Experiments},
  journal = {Quantitative Marketing and Economics},
  year    = {2015},
  volume  = {13},
  number  = {3},
  pages   = {203--247},
  doi     = {10.1007/s11129-015-9159-9}
}
```
Randomized field experiments that vary the temporal spacing of online ad exposures while holding total frequency constant. Finds that spreading the same number of impressions over more days increases purchases — spacing, not just frequency, drives effectiveness — and that ads also work partly by reminding consumers of the advertiser. Direct experimental evidence on advertising scheduling/wear-in in digital media.
**Section:** digital, how-it-works

## Measurement & Field Experiments at Scale

```bibtex
@article{lewis2015unfavorable,
  author  = {Lewis, Randall A. and Rao, Justin M.},
  title   = {The Unfavorable Economics of Measuring the Returns to Advertising},
  journal = {The Quarterly Journal of Economics},
  year    = {2015},
  volume  = {130},
  number  = {4},
  pages   = {1941--1973},
  doi     = {10.1093/qje/qjv023}
}
```
Analyzes 25 large-scale digital advertising field experiments (millions of users) and shows that sales are so volatile relative to ad spend that even experiments with millions of subjects often cannot statistically distinguish a highly profitable campaign from a money-losing one. Formalizes why measuring advertising ROI is intrinsically hard and why observational methods are even worse. The defining statement of the "unfavorable economics" problem.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

```bibtex
@article{gordon2019comparison,
  author  = {Gordon, Brett R. and Zettelmeyer, Florian and Bhargava, Neha and Chapsky, Dan},
  title   = {A Comparison of Approaches to Advertising Measurement: Evidence from Big Field Experiments at Facebook},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {2019},
  volume  = {38},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {193--225},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.2018.1135}
}
```
Compares RCT-based ad lift with common observational/quasi-experimental estimators (matching, regression, propensity scoring) using 15 large Facebook advertising experiments. Finds observational methods frequently fail to recover the experimental ground truth — sometimes off by large factors and even in the wrong direction — because of unobserved targeting and activity bias. The benchmark "observational vs. experimental" comparison for digital advertising.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

```bibtex
@article{gordon2023closeenough,
  author  = {Gordon, Brett R. and Moakler, Robert and Zettelmeyer, Florian},
  title   = {Close Enough? A Large-Scale Exploration of Non-Experimental Approaches to Advertising Measurement},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {2023},
  volume  = {42},
  number  = {4},
  pages   = {768--793},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.2022.1413}
}
```
A much larger follow-up (663 Facebook RCTs) re-examining whether richer data and modern observational methods can approximate experimental lift. Finds that even with extensive user-level features, non-experimental estimates are often not "close enough" for confident decision making, though performance varies systematically with campaign characteristics. Updates and largely reaffirms the case for experimentation in ad measurement.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

```bibtex
@article{lewis2014onlineoffline,
  author  = {Lewis, Randall A. and Reiley, David H.},
  title   = {Online Ads and Offline Sales: Measuring the Effect of Retail Advertising via a Controlled Experiment on {Yahoo!}},
  journal = {Quantitative Marketing and Economics},
  year    = {2014},
  volume  = {12},
  number  = {3},
  pages   = {235--266},
  doi     = {10.1007/s11129-014-9146-6}
}
```
A randomized experiment with 1.6 million customers matched to a major retailer's in-store and online sales, measuring the causal effect of online display advertising on actual purchases. Finds positive and profitable effects concentrated offline and among older customers, with most of the sales lift occurring among people who never clicked the ad. An early demonstration that click-based metrics miss most of advertising's value.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

```bibtex
@article{johnson2017ghostads,
  author  = {Johnson, Garrett A. and Lewis, Randall A. and Nubbemeyer, Elmar I.},
  title   = {Ghost Ads: Improving the Economics of Measuring Online Ad Effectiveness},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {2017},
  volume  = {54},
  number  = {6},
  pages   = {867--884},
  doi     = {10.1509/jmr.15.0297}
}
```
Introduces "ghost ads," a methodology that identifies the counterfactual control group (users who would have been shown the ad) without wasting impressions on costly public-service-announcement controls. This sharply lowers the cost and raises the precision of advertising incrementality experiments and integrates cleanly with real-time ad auctions. A key measurement/incrementality methodology now widely used in industry.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

```bibtex
@article{johnson2017lessismore,
  author  = {Johnson, Garrett A. and Lewis, Randall A. and Reiley, David H.},
  title   = {When Less Is More: Data and Power in Advertising Experiments},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {2017},
  volume  = {36},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {43--53},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.2016.0998}
}
```
Shows that using control ads to identify which users were actually treated lets the analyst discard sales data from unexposed users, boosting statistical power even though it "throws away" data. With a 3-million-user retailer/Yahoo! experiment, control-ad designs improve precision by roughly a third. A practical advance on the power problem that Lewis and Rao formalized.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

```bibtex
@article{berman2018attribution,
  author  = {Berman, Ron},
  title   = {Beyond the Last Touch: Attribution in Online Advertising},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {2018},
  volume  = {37},
  number  = {5},
  pages   = {771--792},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.2018.1104}
}
```
A game-theoretic and empirical analysis of multi-touch attribution showing that the dominant last-touch rule distorts advertiser incentives and publisher behavior, and that Shapley-value attribution can restore efficiency. Demonstrates how the choice of attribution model — not just the data — changes measured channel value. The reference treatment of attribution as a strategic, not merely statistical, problem.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

```bibtex
@article{li2014attribution,
  author  = {Li, Hongshuang (Alice) and Kannan, P. K.},
  title   = {Attributing Conversions in a Multichannel Online Marketing Environment: An Empirical Model and a Field Experiment},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {2014},
  volume  = {51},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {40--56},
  doi     = {10.1509/jmr.13.0050}
}
```
Builds an individual-level model of the online path to purchase that estimates each channel's incremental (carryover- and spillover-adjusted) contribution to conversion, validated against a field experiment. Shows that naive last-click attribution badly misallocates credit across search, display, and email. An empirical complement to Berman on data-driven attribution.
**Section:** measurement/experiments, digital

## Targeting & Privacy Trade-offs

```bibtex
@article{goldfarb2011targeting,
  author  = {Goldfarb, Avi and Tucker, Catherine},
  title   = {Online Display Advertising: Targeting and Obtrusiveness},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {2011},
  volume  = {30},
  number  = {3},
  pages   = {389--404},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.1100.0583}
}
```
Using a large field-experiment dataset of display campaigns, shows that contextual targeting and ad obtrusiveness each raise purchase intent, but combining strong targeting with highly obtrusive (intrusive) formats backfires — consumers react against ads that are both well-targeted and visually aggressive, especially for privacy-sensitive products. Establishes the targeting/obtrusiveness interaction central to display advertising design.
**Section:** targeting-privacy, digital

```bibtex
@article{goldfarb2011privacy,
  author  = {Goldfarb, Avi and Tucker, Catherine E.},
  title   = {Privacy Regulation and Online Advertising},
  journal = {Management Science},
  year    = {2011},
  volume  = {57},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {57--71},
  doi     = {10.1287/mnsc.1100.1246}
}
```
Exploits the EU Privacy and Electronic Communications Directive as a natural experiment, comparing ad effectiveness before/after across European vs. non-European users (3.3 million survey responses, ~9,600 campaigns). Finds that restricting behavioral targeting reduced display-ad effectiveness by about 65%, quantifying the advertising cost of privacy regulation. The foundational empirical estimate of the privacy/effectiveness trade-off.
**Section:** targeting-privacy, digital

```bibtex
@article{lambrecht2013retargeting,
  author  = {Lambrecht, Anja and Tucker, Catherine},
  title   = {When Does Retargeting Work? Information Specificity in Online Advertising},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {2013},
  volume  = {50},
  number  = {5},
  pages   = {561--576},
  doi     = {10.1509/jmr.11.0503}
}
```
A field experiment with an online travel firm comparing generic brand ads against dynamically retargeted product ads. Finds that narrowly retargeting consumers with the specific products they browsed often underperforms generic ads until the consumer has refined their preferences (e.g., after visiting a review site) — highly specific retargeting works only at the right stage of the decision journey. Nuances the "retargeting always wins" intuition.
**Section:** targeting-privacy, digital

```bibtex
@article{johnson2020privacychoice,
  author  = {Johnson, Garrett A. and Shriver, Scott K. and Du, Shaoyin},
  title   = {Consumer Privacy Choice in Online Advertising: Who Opts Out and at What Cost to Industry?},
  journal = {Marketing Science},
  year    = {2020},
  volume  = {39},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {33--51},
  doi     = {10.1287/mksc.2019.1198}
}
```
Uses data from the advertising-industry opt-out program to study who exercises privacy choice and what it costs advertisers. Finds relatively few consumers opt out, but opt-outs are disproportionately valuable users, so the revenue impact per opt-out is large; ad prices for opted-out users fall by roughly half. Quantifies the market consequences of consumer-level privacy choice in advertising.
**Section:** targeting-privacy, digital

```bibtex
@article{fong2015geoconquesting,
  author  = {Fong, Nathan M. and Fang, Zheng and Luo, Xueming},
  title   = {Geo-Conquesting: Competitive Locational Targeting of Mobile Promotions},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {2015},
  volume  = {52},
  number  = {5},
  pages   = {726--735},
  doi     = {10.1509/jmr.14.0229}
}
```
A randomized mobile field experiment testing location-based targeting, including "geo-conquesting" (targeting consumers near a competitor's location). Finds that mobile promotions sent to consumers at a competitor's location can be highly effective, but require deeper discounts than promotions sent near the firm's own location. Direct experimental evidence on mobile/geo-targeting effectiveness and its cost structure.
**Section:** targeting-privacy, digital

## Advertising Content: Creativity, Emotion & Virality

```bibtex
@article{dallolio2023creative,
  author  = {Dall'Olio, Filippo and Vakratsas, Demetrios},
  title   = {The Impact of Advertising Creative Strategy on Advertising Elasticity},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {2023},
  volume  = {87},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {26--44},
  doi     = {10.1177/00222429221074960}
}
```
Links coded creative strategy of 2,251 TV ads (across 91 brands) to estimated advertising elasticities, separating content function (cognition, affect, experience) from execution form. Finds experiential content has the largest positive effect on advertising elasticity, followed by cognitive then affective content — connecting the "creative quality" and elasticity literatures empirically. Bridges advertising content and advertising response.
**Section:** content, elasticity

```bibtex
@article{tellis2019virality,
  author  = {Tellis, Gerard J. and MacInnis, Deborah J. and Tirunillai, Seshadri and Zhang, Yanwei},
  title   = {What Drives Virality (Sharing) of Online Digital Content? The Critical Role of Information, Emotion, and Brand Prominence},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {2019},
  volume  = {83},
  number  = {4},
  pages   = {1--20},
  doi     = {10.1177/0022242919841034}
}
```
Analyzes thousands of YouTube video ads to identify which content features drive sharing. Finds emotional content (especially drama, inspiration, warmth, and surprise) and information drive shares, whereas prominent up-front branding suppresses sharing — creating a tension between virality and brand attribution. The leading empirical study of advertising content and earned-media spread.
**Section:** content, digital

```bibtex
@article{berger2012viral,
  author  = {Berger, Jonah and Milkman, Katherine L.},
  title   = {What Makes Online Content Viral?},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {2012},
  volume  = {49},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {192--205},
  doi     = {10.1509/jmr.10.0353}
}
```
Studies nearly 7,000 New York Times articles to identify what makes content shared. Finds that high-arousal emotions — positive (awe) or negative (anger, anxiety) — increase sharing, whereas low-arousal emotion (sadness) decreases it; positive content is more viral overall and arousal is the key mechanism. The foundational account of the emotional drivers of sharing, widely applied to advertising content.
**Section:** content

## Influencer / Social Advertising

```bibtex
@article{hughes2019influencers,
  author  = {Hughes, Christian and Swaminathan, Vanitha and Brooks, Gillian},
  title   = {Driving Brand Engagement Through Online Social Influencers: An Empirical Investigation of Sponsored Blogging Campaigns},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {2019},
  volume  = {83},
  number  = {5},
  pages   = {78--96},
  doi     = {10.1177/0022242919854374}
}
```
Examines sponsored blogging campaigns and shows that influencer-marketing effectiveness depends on the interplay of platform (blog vs. social network), campaign goal (awareness vs. trial), and post content. For example, high source expertise helps more on hub platforms and for trial-oriented campaigns, while incentives can backfire on intrinsically motivated audiences. An early top-journal empirical study of influencer/sponsored-content effectiveness.
**Section:** content, digital

## Verification Notes

- All 24 DOIs above resolve to the published version-of-record in Crossref; SSRN/NBER preprint DOIs were deliberately excluded in favor of the journal article.
- For Clarke (1976) and Mela, Gupta & Lehmann (1997), Crossref lists multiple DOIs (a JSTOR and a SAGE record). The SAGE publisher DOI is used for Mela et al.; the JSTOR DOI (10.2307/3151017) is the registered Crossref DOI for Clarke. Clarke's end page (357) is from the journal record; Crossref lists the first page (345) only.
- Items considered but **excluded** for being outside the target journal set (Journal of Advertising, Journal of Advertising Research, Journal of Interactive Advertising, Communications of the ACM) or for lacking a published VOR DOI at the time of checking: Wojdynski & Evans (native-ad disclosure), Hu/Lodish/Krieger TV update studies, Bagwell's "Economic Analysis of Advertising" handbook chapter, and Johnson/Shriver GDPR working papers. None are cited here; if needed, they require separate verification.
