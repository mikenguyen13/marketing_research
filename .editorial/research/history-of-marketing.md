Books without a DOI are listed in a separate section with publisher details. Metadata (authors, journal, year, volume/issue, pages) was checked against the Crossref record returned for each work.

## Eras of Marketing Thought

```bibtex
@article{wilkie2003eras,
  author  = {Wilkie, William L. and Moore, Elizabeth S.},
  title   = {Scholarly Research in Marketing: Exploring the ``4 Eras'' of Thought Development},
  journal = {Journal of Public Policy \& Marketing},
  year    = {2003},
  volume  = {22},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {116--146},
  doi     = {10.1509/jppm.22.2.116.17639}
}
```
The anchor piece for the chapter. Wilkie and Moore partition the development of marketing thought into four eras (Founding the Field, 1900–1920; Formalizing the Field, 1920–1950; A Paradigm Shift toward management science and behavioral science, 1950–1980; and the Shift Intensifies / fragmentation, 1980–present). They argue the post-1950 paradigm shift, while productive, narrowed the field's engagement with aggregate marketing systems and public policy. The framework gives the chapter its backbone narrative of how research questions, methods, and audiences changed over a century.
**Section:** eras

```bibtex
@article{wilkie1999contributions,
  author  = {Wilkie, William L. and Moore, Elizabeth S.},
  title   = {Marketing's Contributions to Society},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {1999},
  volume  = {63},
  number  = {4_suppl1},
  pages   = {198--218},
  doi     = {10.1177/00222429990634s118}
}
```
A companion to the "4 Eras" article and an important precursor to it. Wilkie and Moore survey how the aggregate marketing system contributes to social welfare and review the long arc of scholarly attention (and inattention) to those macro contributions. It supplies the macromarketing counterweight to the managerial-era narrative and is useful for framing why the eras framework matters normatively. (Crossref also lists an earlier abstract-only stub at 10.2307/1252112; the SAGE version-of-record DOI above is the citable one.)
**Section:** eras, traditions

```bibtex
@article{wilkie2006macromarketing,
  author  = {Wilkie, William L. and Moore, Elizabeth S.},
  title   = {Macromarketing as a Pillar of Marketing Thought},
  journal = {Journal of Macromarketing},
  year    = {2006},
  volume  = {26},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {224--232},
  doi     = {10.1177/0276146706291067}
}
```
Wilkie and Moore argue that macromarketing — the study of marketing's aggregate systems and societal effects — is not a niche but one of the foundational pillars of marketing thought, co-equal historically with the managerial perspective. The essay extends the eras argument by explaining what the post-1950 paradigm shift pushed to the margins. Good for the chapter's discussion of which intellectual traditions were elevated versus eclipsed.
**Section:** eras, traditions

```bibtex
@article{kotler1969broadening,
  author  = {Kotler, Philip and Levy, Sidney J.},
  title   = {Broadening the Concept of Marketing},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {1969},
  volume  = {33},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {10--15},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224296903300103}
}
```
The landmark "broadening" manifesto that pushed marketing's domain beyond profit-seeking firms to nonprofits, ideas, and social causes — a defining moment in the managerial/behavioral era and a trigger for decades of definitional debate. It directly seeds the later AMA-definition controversies and the disciplinary identity question of "what is marketing the study of." Essential as an era-marker showing the scope of marketing thought expanding in real time.
**Section:** eras, schools

```bibtex
@article{vargo2004newlogic,
  author  = {Vargo, Stephen L. and Lusch, Robert F.},
  title   = {Evolving to a New Dominant Logic for Marketing},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {2004},
  volume  = {68},
  number  = {1},
  pages   = {1--17},
  doi     = {10.1509/jmkg.68.1.1.24036}
}
```
Vargo and Lusch articulate "service-dominant logic," arguing marketing thought has been shifting from a goods-centered, exchange-of-tangibles model to one centered on service provision, operant resources, value co-creation, and relationships. It is the most cited recent attempt to name a paradigm-level transition, making it a natural endpoint for the eras narrative and a live example of how a "school" forms in real time. Useful for showing that the historiography of marketing is still being actively contested and written.
**Section:** eras, schools

## Schools of Marketing Thought

```bibtex
@article{shaw2005schools,
  author  = {Shaw, Eric H. and Jones, D. G. Brian},
  title   = {A History of Schools of Marketing Thought},
  journal = {Marketing Theory},
  year    = {2005},
  volume  = {5},
  number  = {3},
  pages   = {239--281},
  doi     = {10.1177/1470593105054898}
}
```
The most thorough single article surveying the "schools" approach to marketing's intellectual history. Shaw and Jones trace roughly ten schools (e.g., commodity, functional, regional, institutional, managerial, buyer behavior, macromarketing, exchange) from the early 20th century onward, classifying them along economic-versus-noneconomic and micro-versus-macro dimensions, and conclude the field stands "at a crossroads." It is the indispensable secondary source for organizing the schools section.
**Section:** schools, traditions

```bibtex
@incollection{jones2011modernschools,
  author    = {Jones, D. G. Brian and Shaw, Eric H. and McLean, Paula A.},
  title     = {The Modern Schools of Marketing Thought},
  booktitle = {The SAGE Handbook of Marketing Theory},
  year      = {2011},
  pages     = {42--58},
  doi       = {10.4135/9781446222454.n3}
}
```
A handbook chapter updating and condensing the schools taxonomy for the modern (post-1950) period, complementary to Shaw and Jones (2005). It pairs with a companion "Early Schools" chapter (DOI 10.4135/9781446222454.n2) by the same authors, which is also Crossref-verified if the chapter needs both halves. Useful as a textbook-level synthesis to cite alongside the longer journal treatment.
**Section:** schools

```bibtex
@article{hunt1976nature,
  author  = {Hunt, Shelby D.},
  title   = {The Nature and Scope of Marketing},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {1976},
  volume  = {40},
  number  = {3},
  pages   = {17--28},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224297604000304}
}
```
Hunt's "three dichotomies" model (profit/nonprofit, micro/macro, positive/normative) became the canonical map of marketing's domain and a direct response to the broadening debate. It is foundational for the schools section because it offers a meta-framework into which the various schools and definitions can be slotted. Frequently cited as the moment marketing theory acquired a self-conscious scope.
**Section:** schools, traditions

```bibtex
@article{hunt1983general,
  author  = {Hunt, Shelby D.},
  title   = {General Theories and the Fundamental Explananda of Marketing},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {1983},
  volume  = {47},
  number  = {4},
  pages   = {9--17},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224298304700402}
}
```
Hunt asks whether a general theory of marketing is possible and specifies the field's "fundamental explananda" — the core phenomena any general theory would have to explain (e.g., why buyers and sellers engage in exchange, why marketing institutions arise). It is a key statement of marketing's aspiration to theoretical maturity and bridges the schools and traditions threads. Pairs naturally with Hunt (1976).
**Section:** schools, traditions

```bibtex
@article{hunt1990truth,
  author  = {Hunt, Shelby D.},
  title   = {Truth in Marketing Theory and Research},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing},
  year    = {1990},
  volume  = {54},
  number  = {3},
  pages   = {1--15},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224299005400301}
}
```
Hunt's defense of scientific realism against the relativist/constructivist "crisis literature" of the 1980s, arguing that truth, objectivity, and progress remain coherent goals for marketing research. This is the central text for the chapter's treatment of marketing's philosophy-of-science debates and Hunt's realist tradition. It also documents a genuine intellectual schism in the discipline, useful for the sociology angle on how marketing argues about its own legitimacy.
**Section:** schools, traditions

## Definitions of Marketing Over Time (AMA Definition Debates)

```bibtex
@article{gundlach2009amadefinition,
  author  = {Gundlach, Gregory T. and Wilkie, William L.},
  title   = {The American Marketing Association's New Definition of Marketing: Perspective and Commentary on the 2007 Revision},
  journal = {Journal of Public Policy \& Marketing},
  year    = {2009},
  volume  = {28},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {259--264},
  doi     = {10.1509/jppm.28.2.259}
}
```
A focused commentary on the AMA's 2007 official definition of marketing, situating it against the 2004 revision and earlier definitions. Gundlach and Wilkie evaluate what the new wording includes and omits (notably its treatment of value, exchange, and society) and what that signals about the discipline's self-conception. The anchor source for the definitions-debate portion of the chapter.
**Section:** eras, schools, sociology

```bibtex
@article{gundlach2007ama2004,
  author  = {Gundlach, Gregory T.},
  title   = {The American Marketing Association's 2004 Definition of Marketing: Perspectives on Its Implications for Scholarship and the Role and Responsibility of Marketing in Society},
  journal = {Journal of Public Policy \& Marketing},
  year    = {2007},
  volume  = {26},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {243--250},
  doi     = {10.1509/jppm.26.2.243}
}
```
Gundlach examines the AMA's controversial 2004 definition — which recentered marketing on "creating, communicating, and delivering value" and on managing customer relationships — and its implications for scholarship and for marketing's societal role. It documents the scholarly pushback that the 2004 wording was too managerial and narrow, motivating the 2007 revision. Use it to give the definitions debate its before-and-after structure.
**Section:** eras, schools, sociology

```bibtex
@article{zinkhan2007altassessment,
  author  = {Zinkhan, George M. and Williams, Brian C.},
  title   = {The New American Marketing Association Definition of Marketing: An Alternative Assessment},
  journal = {Journal of Public Policy \& Marketing},
  year    = {2007},
  volume  = {26},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {284--288},
  doi     = {10.1509/jppm.26.2.284}
}
```
A dissenting commentary arguing that the AMA's revised definition fails to capture the full expanse of contemporary marketing thought and practice. It is valuable for showing that the definition debates were genuinely contested rather than settled, and for representing voices skeptical of official codification. Pairs with the two Gundlach pieces to give a balanced view of the controversy.
**Section:** schools, sociology

## Marketing Science / The Quantitative Turn (Retrospectives)

```bibtex
@incollection{lilien1994models,
  author    = {Lilien, Gary L.},
  title     = {Marketing Models: Past, Present and Future},
  booktitle = {Research Traditions in Marketing},
  series    = {International Series in Quantitative Marketing},
  year      = {1994},
  pages     = {1--26},
  doi       = {10.1007/978-94-011-1402-8_1}
}
```
Lilien's retrospective chronicles the rise of the marketing-models / marketing-science tradition across three periods, from early operations-research applications through the scanner-data and econometric era. Appearing in the aptly titled *Research Traditions in Marketing* volume, it doubles as a primary statement of the quantitative tradition's self-understanding. Excellent for the chapter's treatment of the modeling turn and how it institutionalized as a distinct subculture.
**Section:** traditions, eras

```bibtex
@article{guadagni2008logit25,

  number  = {1},
```

The authors' own retrospective on their 1983 scanner-data logit model, one of the most consequential empirical papers of the quantitative turn. It reflects on how a methodological innovation diffused into both academia and practice and why its impact surprised the authors. A concrete, citable example of how the modeling era's signature methods took hold.
**Section:** traditions

Useful as a primary-source snapshot of how the marketing-science community narrates its own history and standards. Best cited as a brief institutional reflection rather than a substantive survey.

## Sociology of the Discipline (Doctoral Training, Top Journals, Placement, Open Science / Replication)

```bibtex
@article{baumgartner2003structural,
  author  = {Baumgartner, Hans and Pieters, Rik},
  title   = {The Structural Influence of Marketing Journals: A Citation Analysis of the Discipline and Its Subareas over Time},

  number  = {2},
  pages   = {123--139},

```
A citation analysis mapping the influence structure of marketing journals over three decades, quantifying which journals (and which the "top-4" core) anchor knowledge flows in the discipline and its subfields. It is the key empirical source for the chapter's claims about journal prestige hierarchies and how influence concentrates. Directly supports the sociology-of-the-field discussion of the top journals.
**Section:** sociology

```bibtex
@article{davis2005doctoral,

  journal = {Journal of Marketing Education},

  pages   = {14--24},
  doi     = {10.1177/0273475304268783}

```
Documents a sharp decline in new marketing doctorates (a 32% drop in PhD production between the mid-1990s and early 2000s) and analyzes the recruiting pipeline into doctoral programs. It is a concrete data point for the doctoral-pipeline portion of the sociology section and a window into how the field reproduces itself. Useful for discussing supply, demand, and the academic labor market.
**Section:** sociology

```bibtex
@article{cheng2003productivity,
  author  = {Cheng, Louis T. W. and Chan, Kam C. and Chan, Ricky Y. K.},
  title   = {Publications in Major Marketing Journals: An Analysis of Research Productivity of Asia-Pacific Universities},

  number  = {2},
  pages   = {163--176},
  doi     = {10.1177/0273475303254023}
}
```
A productivity-ranking study counting publications across 20 marketing journals to rank institutions, illustrating the bibliometric "counting games" by which marketing departments and faculty are evaluated and placed. It exemplifies the metrics culture that shapes hiring, tenure, and placement in the field. Cite it as an example of how productivity rankings operate (and their regional/journal-weighting choices), rather than for its specific rankings.
**Section:** sociology

```bibtex
@article{sawyer1983significance,
  author  = {Sawyer, Alan G. and Peter, J. Paul},
  title   = {The Significance of Statistical Significance Tests in Marketing Research},
  journal = {Journal of Marketing Research},
  year    = {1983},
  volume  = {20},
  number  = {2},
  pages   = {122--133},
  doi     = {10.1177/002224378302000203}
}
```
An early and prescient critique arguing that classical significance testing was misinterpreted and overvalued in marketing, advocating replication, effect sizes, Bayesian inference, and meta-analysis. It anticipates by decades the methodological reforms now grouped under "open science," making it the historical anchor for the replication thread. Shows the field worried about inferential rigor long before the broader replication crisis.
**Section:** sociology, traditions

```bibtex
@article{lynch2015replication,
  author  = {Lynch, John G. and Bradlow, Eric T. and Huber, Joel C. and Lehmann, Donald R.},
  title   = {Reflections on the Replication Corner: In Praise of Conceptual Replications},
  journal = {International Journal of Research in Marketing},
  year    = {2015},
  volume  = {32},
  number  = {4},
  pages   = {333--342},
  doi     = {10.1016/j.ijresmar.2015.09.006}
}
```
Reflecting on IJRM's "Replication Corner," these senior scholars argue for the distinctive value of conceptual replications (testing a theory across new operationalizations and contexts) over strict direct replications. It is a marketing-specific contribution to the replication/open-science debate and reveals how the field's leadership frames reproducibility on its own terms. Central to the sociology section's open-science discussion.
**Section:** sociology
