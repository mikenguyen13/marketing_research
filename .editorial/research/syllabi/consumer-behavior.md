# Consumer Behavior (Behavioral) PhD Seminar — Canonical Full-Semester Structure

A synthesized, build-ready structure for a ~14-week doctoral seminar in **Consumer Behavior** (the behavioral / consumer-psychology track of a marketing PhD program), modeled on the way the top programs (Wharton, Chicago Booth, Stanford GSB, Michigan Ross, Columbia, NYU Stern, Duke Fuqua, Ohio State Fisher, and peers) organize the course.

**Verification protocol.** Every reading below carries a DOI that was matched against the Crossref REST API (`https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=...`) and resolved against the version-of-record metadata (`https://api.crossref.org/works/<doi>`); title, authors, journal, year, volume, issue, and pages were checked against the returned record. **No DOI or paper here is invented.** Where a candidate could not be verified to a published version-of-record, it was omitted rather than guessed. Verified on: 2026-06-21.

**Access caveat (important, for the human editor).** In this environment, live program/syllabus URLs and general web search were permission-denied; only `api.crossref.org` was reachable. I therefore could **not** load any program's actual syllabus PDF or reading list, and I do **not** attribute specific week numbers, course numbers, or verbatim reading choices to any specific program. The weekly architecture below is built from the **well-established canonical structure of the consumer-behavior field** — a structure that is stable across programs and decades — and every reading is independently DOI-verified. The "Sources consulted" section lists program landing pages as pointers for a human (or a future web-enabled run) to confirm and extend the specifics; it does **not** claim those pages were fetched.

## 1. Semester arc

A consumer-behavior doctoral seminar is, at its core, an apprenticeship in **psychological theory applied to consumption** and in the **experimental craft** used to test it. The arc therefore moves on two tracks at once. Substantively, it begins with the *architecture of the mind* — how consumers attend to, encode, and process information; how they form attitudes; and how they make choices — and then layers on *the forces that bend that architecture*: heuristics and biases, affect and emotion, motivation and goals, self-control, the self and identity, and finally the social and cultural context in which all consumption is embedded. Methodologically, the seminar runs a parallel spine: from the logic of theory-driven experimentation and process evidence (mediation/moderation), through the credibility revolution (the replication crisis, pre-registration, and the shift toward more robust designs), and out to the frontier where lab experiments meet field data, large behavioral datasets, and consumer–technology interaction.

The intellectual logic is **cumulative and dialectical**. Week 1 establishes consumer research as a distinct discipline poised between psychology and economics, and frames the seminar's recurring tension: the *rational-economic* model of the consumer versus the *psychological-constructive* model in which preferences are built on the spot, context-dependent, and shaped by affect and identity. Each subsequent module is a refinement of, or a challenge to, that opening tension. Information processing and dual-process models (Weeks 2–3) explain *how* judgments are formed; behavioral decision theory and prospect theory (Weeks 4–5) show *how systematically they depart* from normative benchmarks; affect, motivation, and self-control (Weeks 6–8) supply the *drivers* behind those departures; the self, identity, and social/cultural influence (Weeks 9–11) embed the individual consumer in a web of meaning and other people; and the closing modules (Weeks 12–14) turn to consumer well-being, the methodological reckoning of the open-science era, and the technological frontier (AI, algorithms, robots) that is reshaping what "a consumer decision" even is.

By design, the seminar is **method-forward as well as theory-forward**: students are expected to leave able to *read a JCR/JCP/JMR behavioral paper critically, identify its theoretical contribution and its process account, evaluate the cleanliness of its experimental identification, and design a study that extends it.* Most programs pair the readings with a term-long requirement to develop an original research proposal (an "idea paper" or a short empirical paper), so the weekly debates are taught not as settled history but as live research frontiers a first- or second-year student could plausibly enter.

## 2. Weekly modules

Each module lists the topic, sub-topics, key readings (`@key` + full reference + verified DOI + why it is canonical), the methods/skills emphasized, and the central debate(s). **[F]** marks a *foundational classic*; **[R]** marks *recent/frontier work* (roughly last 5 years).

### Week 1 — Foundations: What Is Consumer Research? Rational vs. Constructive Consumers

**Sub-topics:** the identity of consumer behavior as a field (psychology ↔ economics ↔ marketing); the normative (utility-maximizing) consumer vs. the descriptive/constructive consumer; preference construction; levels of explanation.

**Key readings**

- `@bettman1998constructive` **[F]** — Bettman, James R., Mary Frances Luce, and John W. Payne (1998), "Constructive Consumer Choice Processes," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 25 (3), 187–217. DOI: `10.1086/209535`. *Why canonical:* the field's manifesto for the view that consumers build preferences on the spot using context-sensitive, effort-accuracy-trading strategies rather than reading off stable utilities — the organizing frame for the whole seminar.
- `@simonson1989reasons` **[F]** — Simonson, Itamar (1989), "Choice Based on Reasons: The Case of Attraction and Compromise Effects," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 16 (2), 158–174. DOI: `10.1086/209205`. *Why canonical:* the foundational demonstration that adding a third option shifts choice shares, establishing context-dependent (not context-free) preference as the norm.
- `@simonson1992context` **[F]** — Simonson, Itamar, and Amos Tversky (1992), "Choice in Context: Tradeoff Contrast and Extremeness Aversion," *Journal of Marketing Research*, 29 (3), 281–295. DOI: `10.1177/002224379202900301`. *Why canonical:* generalizes context effects into tradeoff contrast and extremeness aversion, the most-cited bridge from psychology to assortment/line-design strategy.

**Methods/skills:** reading a behavioral paper for its theoretical claim and contribution; the normative-vs-descriptive distinction; what counts as "a consumer behavior contribution."

**Central debate(s):** Are preferences *revealed* (stable, pre-existing) or *constructed* (built in the moment)? Is consumer behavior an application of psychology or a theory-generating discipline in its own right?

---

### Week 2 — Information Processing, Attention, Memory, and Categorization

**Sub-topics:** the consumer as information processor; memory and inference; how products/brands are categorized; typicality and its consequences.

**Key readings**

- `@loken1990typicality` **[F]** — Loken, Barbara, and James Ward (1990), "Alternative Approaches to Understanding the Determinants of Typicality," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 17 (2), 111–126. DOI: `10.1086/208542`. *Why canonical:* the reference treatment of how consumers categorize products and why typicality (vs. ideals, goals, attribute structure) governs evaluation and inference.
- `@petty1983routes` **[F]** — Petty, Richard E., John T. Cacioppo, and David Schumann (1983), "Central and Peripheral Routes to Advertising Effectiveness: The Moderating Role of Involvement," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 10 (2), 135–146. DOI: `10.1086/208954`. *Why canonical:* imports the Elaboration Likelihood Model into consumer research; the canonical dual-route account of how involvement changes *what* information persuades.
- `@kolbe1991content` — Kolbe, Richard H., and Melissa S. Burnett (1991), "Content-Analysis Research: An Examination of Applications with Directives for Improving Research Reliability and Objectivity," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 18 (2), 243–250. DOI: `10.1086/209256`. *Why canonical:* a methods anchor for coding qualitative/stimulus content reliably — useful when the seminar pairs cognition with measurement of message content.

**Methods/skills:** stimulus design; memory and recall measures; coding/content analysis and intercoder reliability; manipulating involvement and elaboration.

**Central debate(s):** Is persuasion one process with two routes (ELM) or a continuum? How much of "judgment" is online vs. memory-based?

---

### Week 3 — Attitudes, Persuasion, and Dual-Process Theories

**Sub-topics:** attitude formation and change; dual-process / dual-systems accounts; affect vs. cognition in evaluation; argument quality vs. peripheral cues.

**Key readings**

- `@shiv1999heart` **[F]** — Shiv, Baba, and Alexander Fedorikhin (1999), "Heart and Mind in Conflict: The Interplay of Affect and Cognition in Consumer Decision Making," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 26 (3), 278–292. DOI: `10.1086/209563`. *Why canonical:* the seminal consumer-behavior operationalization of the dual-system view — affect wins under cognitive load, deliberation wins under capacity.
- `@petty1983routes` **[F]** — (carried from Week 2 as the dual-process anchor; see above). DOI: `10.1086/208954`.
- `@pham1998feelings` **[F]** — Pham, Michel Tuan (1998), "Representativeness, Relevance, and the Use of Feelings in Decision Making," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 25 (2), 144–159. DOI: `10.1086/209532`. *Why canonical:* specifies *when* consumers rely on feelings as inputs to judgment (the feelings-as-information program applied to consumption).

**Methods/skills:** affect manipulations; cognitive-load designs; distinguishing dual-process from single-process explanations; process dissociation logic.

**Central debate(s):** Two systems or one? Is affect an input to evaluation, an output, or both? When are feelings used as information vs. discounted?

---

### Week 4 — Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics, Biases, and Numerical Cognition

**Sub-topics:** the heuristics-and-biases program; anchoring, availability, representativeness; price and numerical cognition.

**Key readings**

- `@tversky1974heuristics` **[F]** — Tversky, Amos, and Daniel Kahneman (1974), "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science*, 185 (4157), 1124–1131. DOI: `10.1126/science.185.4157.1124`. *Why canonical:* the founding statement of the heuristics-and-biases tradition that underpins behavioral decision research in marketing.
- `@thomas2005leftdigit` **[F]** — Thomas, Manoj, and Vicki Morwitz (2005), "Penny Wise and Pound Foolish: The Left-Digit Effect in Price Cognition," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 32 (1), 54–64. DOI: `10.1086/429600`. *Why canonical:* the definitive demonstration that consumers encode prices by the left-most digit — a clean, replicable bias with direct pricing implications.
- `@sokolova2020leftdigit` **[R]** — Sokolova, Tatiana, Satheesh Seenivasan, and Manoj Thomas (2020), "The Left-Digit Bias: When and Why Are Consumers Penny Wise and Pound Foolish?" *Journal of Marketing Research*, 57 (4), 771–788. DOI: `10.1177/0022243720932532`. *Why canonical (frontier):* establishes the moderators and field generalizability of the left-digit effect, a model of replication-plus-extension.

**Methods/skills:** within-subject vs. between-subject bias demonstrations; numeric stimulus design; field validation of a lab effect.

**Central debate(s):** Are heuristics evidence of irrationality or of ecologically rational, fast-and-frugal cognition? How much do biases survive market discipline and learning?

---

### Week 5 — Behavioral Decision Theory: Prospect Theory, Framing, and Mental Accounting

**Sub-topics:** prospect theory and loss aversion; reference dependence and the endowment effect; framing; mental accounting and transaction utility.

**Key readings**

- `@kahneman1979prospect` **[F]** — Kahneman, Daniel, and Amos Tversky (1979), "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk," *Econometrica*, 47 (2), 263–291. DOI: `10.2307/1914185`. *Why canonical:* the cornerstone descriptive theory of choice under risk (value function, loss aversion, probability weighting) on which a generation of consumer research is built.
- `@thaler1985mental` **[F]** — Thaler, Richard (1985), "Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice," *Marketing Science*, 4 (3), 199–214. DOI: `10.1287/mksc.4.3.199`. *Why canonical:* introduces transaction utility and mental accounting to marketing — the bridge from prospect theory to everyday consumer purchase decisions.
- `@kahneman1990endowment` **[F]** — Kahneman, Daniel, Jack L. Knetsch, and Richard H. Thaler (1990), "Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem," *Journal of Political Economy*, 98 (6), 1325–1348. DOI: `10.1086/261737`. *Why canonical:* the experimental proof of loss aversion in exchange (the "mug" experiments), foundational for WTA/WTP gaps and ownership effects.

**Methods/skills:** designing risky-choice and framing manipulations; eliciting WTA/WTP; reference-point manipulation; reading formal value-function notation.

**Central debate(s):** Is loss aversion a robust, near-universal regularity or context-bound (and partly a measurement artifact)? Do mental accounts describe cognition or merely fit behavior?

---

### Week 6 — Affect and Emotion in Consumption

**Sub-topics:** mood-as-information; valence vs. discrete emotions; appraisal-tendency framework; incidental vs. integral affect.

**Key readings**

- `@schwarz1983mood` **[F]** — Schwarz, Norbert, and Gerald L. Clore (1983), "Mood, Misattribution, and Judgments of Well-Being: Informative and Directive Functions of Affective States," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 45 (3), 513–523. DOI: `10.1037/0022-3514.45.3.513`. *Why canonical:* the founding feelings-as-information / mood-misattribution paradigm imported throughout consumer affect research.
- `@lerner2000beyond` **[F]** — Lerner, Jennifer S., and Dacher Keltner (2000), "Beyond Valence: Toward a Model of Emotion-Specific Influences on Judgement and Choice," *Cognition & Emotion*, 14 (4), 473–493. DOI: `10.1080/026999300402763`. *Why canonical:* the appraisal-tendency framework establishing that *specific* emotions (fear vs. anger), not just valence, have distinct, predictable effects on judgment.
- `@shiv1999heart` **[F]** — (also fits here as the affect-vs-cognition anchor; see Week 3). DOI: `10.1086/209563`.

**Methods/skills:** incidental-affect inductions; misattribution paradigms; measuring discrete emotions; separating integral from incidental affect.

**Central debate(s):** Is affect organized by valence/arousal or by discrete, appraisal-specified emotions? When does emotion improve vs. degrade decision quality?

---

### Week 7 — Motivation, Goals, and Regulatory Focus

**Sub-topics:** regulatory focus (promotion/prevention) and regulatory fit; goal activation, pursuit, and balancing; self-construal × motivation.

**Key readings**

- `@higgins1997beyond` **[F]** — Higgins, E. Tory (1997), "Beyond Pleasure and Pain," *American Psychologist*, 52 (12), 1280–1300. DOI: `10.1037/0003-066x.52.12.1280`. *Why canonical:* the founding statement of regulatory focus theory (promotion vs. prevention) that pervades consumer motivation and message-framing research.
- `@aaker2001pleasures` **[F]** — Aaker, Jennifer L., and Angela Y. Lee (2001), "'I' Seek Pleasures and 'We' Avoid Pains: The Role of Self-Regulatory Goals in Information Processing and Persuasion," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 28 (1), 33–49. DOI: `10.1086/321946`. *Why canonical:* the canonical consumer demonstration that self-construal activates promotion/prevention goals that change which appeals persuade.
- `@fishbach2005goals` **[F]** — Fishbach, Ayelet, and Ravi Dhar (2005), "Goals as Excuses or Guides: The Liberating Effect of Perceived Goal Progress on Choice," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 32 (3), 370–377. DOI: `10.1086/497548`. *Why canonical:* the touchstone for goal-balancing/licensing — progress on a goal can *release* incongruent (indulgent) choice.

**Methods/skills:** priming goals and self-construal; manipulating regulatory focus and fit; measuring goal commitment and progress.

**Central debate(s):** Do goals operate as guides (commitment, highlighting) or as excuses (licensing, balancing)? Is regulatory fit a value-from-fit effect or a fluency/affect artifact?

---

### Week 8 — Self-Control, Intertemporal Choice, and Hedonic vs. Utilitarian Consumption

**Sub-topics:** willpower and ego depletion; precommitment and self-rationing; vice/virtue and justification; hedonic vs. utilitarian goods.

**Key readings**

- `@baumeister1998ego` **[F]** — Baumeister, Roy F., Ellen Bratslavsky, Mark Muraven, and Dianne M. Tice (1998), "Ego Depletion: Is the Active Self a Limited Resource?" *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 74 (5), 1252–1265. DOI: `10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1252`. *Why canonical:* the founding "strength model" of self-control; also a live case study in the replication debate (see Week 13).
- `@wertenbroch1998rationing` **[F]** — Wertenbroch, Klaus (1998), "Consumption Self-Control by Rationing Purchase Quantities of Virtue and Vice," *Marketing Science*, 17 (4), 317–337. DOI: `10.1287/mksc.17.4.317`. *Why canonical:* shows consumers strategically *ration* vice goods to control future consumption — self-control as a market behavior.
- `@ariely2002precommitment` **[F]** — Ariely, Dan, and Klaus Wertenbroch (2002), "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance: Self-Control by Precommitment," *Psychological Science*, 13 (3), 219–224. DOI: `10.1111/1467-9280.00441`. *Why canonical:* clean field+lab evidence that people impose costly deadlines on themselves to combat present bias.
- `@dhar2000hedonic` **[F]** — Dhar, Ravi, and Klaus Wertenbroch (2000), "Consumer Choice between Hedonic and Utilitarian Goods," *Journal of Marketing Research*, 37 (1), 60–71. DOI: `10.1509/jmkr.37.1.60.18718`. *Why canonical:* the standard reference for the hedonic/utilitarian distinction and how it asymmetrically governs acquisition vs. forfeiture choices.

**Methods/skills:** depletion manipulations; intertemporal-choice and discounting measures; field self-control designs; classifying goods as hedonic vs. utilitarian.

**Central debate(s):** Is self-control a depletable resource, a motivational/attentional shift, or a process artifact (the depletion replication controversy)? Do consumers need *more* justification to choose hedonic goods?

---

### Week 9 — The Self, Identity, and Possessions

**Sub-topics:** the extended self; self-discrepancy and self-completion; identity-based consumption; compensatory consumption.

**Key readings**

- `@belk1988extendedself` **[F]** — Belk, Russell W. (1988), "Possessions and the Extended Self," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 15 (2), 139–168. DOI: `10.1086/209154`. *Why canonical:* the field-defining statement that we are what we own; the conceptual root of identity, attachment, and meaning-of-possessions research.
- `@higgins1987selfdiscrepancy` **[F]** — Higgins, E. Tory (1987), "Self-Discrepancy: A Theory Relating Self and Affect," *Psychological Review*, 94 (3), 319–340. DOI: `10.1037/0033-295X.94.3.319`. *Why canonical:* supplies the actual/ideal/ought-self machinery behind self-gaps that motivate consumption.
- `@rucker2008desire` **[F]** — Rucker, Derek D., and Adam D. Galinsky (2008), "Desire to Acquire: Powerlessness and Compensatory Consumption," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 35 (2), 257–267. DOI: `10.1086/588569`. *Why canonical:* the foundational link from a threatened/low-power self-state to status-restoring consumption.

**Methods/skills:** self-threat manipulations; measuring self-construal and self-discrepancy; symbolic self-completion designs.

**Central debate(s):** Do possessions *constitute* the self or merely *signal* it? Is compensatory consumption about the specific deficit (symbolic self-completion) or general affect repair?

### Week 10 — Social Influence, Norms, and Word of Mouth

**Sub-topics:** descriptive vs. injunctive norms; conformity vs. divergence; identity signaling; social transmission and virality.

**Key readings**

- `@schultz2007norms` **[F]** — Schultz, P. Wesley, Jessica M. Nolan, Robert B. Cialdini, Noah J. Goldstein, and Vladas Griskevicius (2007), "The Constructive, Destructive, and Reconstructive Power of Social Norms," *Psychological Science*, 18 (5), 429–434. DOI: `10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01917.x`. *Why canonical:* the field experiment showing descriptive norms can backfire and that *injunctive* norms correct the boomerang — the workhorse norms paper.
- `@berger2007diverge` **[F]** — Berger, Jonah, and Chip Heath (2007), "Where Consumers Diverge from Others: Identity Signaling and Product Domains," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 34 (2), 121–134. DOI: `10.1086/519142`. *Why canonical:* explains when consumers *diverge* to protect identity rather than conform — central to taste, adoption, and abandonment.
- `@berger2012viral` **[R-ish]** — Berger, Jonah, and Katherine L. Milkman (2012), "What Makes Online Content Viral?" *Journal of Marketing Research*, 49 (2), 192–205. DOI: `10.1509/jmr.10.0353`. *Why canonical:* the founding empirical account of social transmission — high-arousal emotion drives sharing — bridging psychology and digital word of mouth.

**Methods/skills:** norm-message field experiments; archival/text analysis of sharing; manipulating identity relevance and observability.

**Central debate(s):** When do consumers conform vs. diverge? Are virality and word of mouth driven by emotion (arousal), content utility, or network structure?

---

### Week 11 — Status, Signaling, and Conspicuous Consumption (Behavioral Economics Meets Identity)

**Sub-topics:** status signaling and "loud vs. quiet" luxury; need for status; signaling and counter-signaling.

**Key readings**

- `@han2010signaling` **[F]** — Han, Young Jee, Joseph C. Nunes, and Xavier Drèze (2010), "Signaling Status with Luxury Goods: The Role of Brand Prominence," *Journal of Marketing*, 74 (4), 15–30. DOI: `10.1509/jmkg.74.4.015`. *Why canonical:* the taxonomy (Patricians/Parvenus/Poseurs/Proletarians) linking wealth, need for status, and preference for loud vs. quiet brand prominence.
- `@berger2007diverge` **[F]** — (also central here as the identity-signaling anchor; see Week 10). DOI: `10.1086/519142`.
- `@belk1988extendedself` **[F]** — (the symbolic-consumption foundation underlying status display; see Week 9). DOI: `10.1086/209154`.

**Methods/skills:** signaling-game intuition for behavioral researchers; manipulating observability/conspicuousness; measuring need for status.

**Central debate(s):** Is conspicuous consumption about signaling to others (economic) or about constructing the self (psychological)? When does counter-signaling ("quiet luxury") dominate?

### Week 12 — Culture, Money, Time, and Consumer Well-Being

**Sub-topics:** cultural self-construal; money vs. time as frames; experiential vs. material purchases; prosocial spending and sustainable consumption.

**Key readings**

- `@markus1991culture` **[F]** — Markus, Hazel R., and Shinobu Kitayama (1991), "Culture and the Self: Implications for Cognition, Emotion, and Motivation," *Psychological Review*, 98 (2), 224–253. DOI: `10.1037/0033-295X.98.2.224`. *Why canonical:* the independent/interdependent self-construal framework that grounds essentially all cross-cultural consumer research.
- `@mogilner2009timemoney` **[F]** — Mogilner, Cassie, and Jennifer Aaker (2009), "The Time vs. Money Effect: Shifting Product Attitudes and Decisions through Personal Connection," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 36 (2), 277–291. DOI: `10.1086/597161`. *Why canonical:* shows that priming *time* (vs. money) deepens personal connection and improves attitudes — a touchstone for consumer well-being research.
- `@vanboven2003todo` **[F]** — Van Boven, Leaf, and Thomas Gilovich (2003), "To Do or to Have? That Is the Question," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 85 (6), 1193–1202. DOI: `10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193`. *Why canonical:* the founding experiential-over-material happiness result that launched the experiential-purchase literature.
- `@dunn2008spending` **[F]** — Dunn, Elizabeth W., Lara B. Aknin, and Michael I. Norton (2008), "Spending Money on Others Promotes Happiness," *Science*, 319 (5870), 1687–1688. DOI: `10.1126/science.1150952`. *Why canonical:* the prosocial-spending result connecting consumption to well-being; widely taught and (instructively) part of the replication conversation.
- `@white2019shift` **[R]** — White, Katherine, Rishad Habib, and David J. Hardisty (2019), "How to SHIFT Consumer Behaviors to be More Sustainable: A Literature Review and Guiding Framework," *Journal of Marketing*, 83 (3), 22–49. DOI: `10.1177/0022242919825649`. *Why canonical (frontier):* the integrative SHIFT framework that has become the reference point for sustainable-consumption research and transformative consumer research (TCR).

**Methods/skills:** cross-cultural design and self-construal priming; well-being/affect measurement; field interventions for behavior change.

**Central debate(s):** Are cultural differences in consumption traceable to a single self-construal dimension? Does money buy happiness when spent on experiences/others — and how robust are these effects?

### Week 13 — Research Methods, Process Evidence, and the Credibility Revolution

**Sub-topics:** experimental design and identification of process (mediation vs. moderation-of-process); the replication crisis; questionable research practices; pre-registration and open science.

**Key readings**

- `@spencer2005causalchain` **[F]** — Spencer, Steven J., Mark P. Zanna, and Geoffrey T. Fong (2005), "Establishing a Causal Chain: Why Experiments Are Often More Effective Than Mediational Analyses in Examining Psychological Processes," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 89 (6), 845–851. DOI: `10.1037/0022-3514.89.6.845`. *Why canonical:* the standard argument for *experimentally manipulating the mediator* (moderation-of-process) rather than relying on statistical mediation alone.
- `@zhao2010mediation` **[F]** — Zhao, Xinshu, John G. Lynch Jr., and Qimei Chen (2010), "Reconsidering Baron and Kenny: Myths and Truths about Mediation Analysis," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 37 (2), 197–206. DOI: `10.1086/651257`. *Why canonical:* the most-cited mediation-methods reference in consumer research; reframes mediation around the indirect effect and bootstrapping.
- `@simmons2011falsepositive` **[F]** — Simmons, Joseph P., Leif D. Nelson, and Uri Simonsohn (2011), "False-Positive Psychology: Undisclosed Flexibility in Data Collection and Analysis Allows Presenting Anything as Significant," *Psychological Science*, 22 (11), 1359–1366. DOI: `10.1177/0956797611417632`. *Why canonical:* the paper that named "researcher degrees of freedom" and catalyzed pre-registration and disclosure norms.
- `@osc2015reproducibility` **[R-anchor]** — Open Science Collaboration (2015), "Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science," *Science*, 349 (6251), aac4716. DOI: `10.1126/science.aac4716`. *Why canonical:* the large-scale replication project that quantified the crisis and reset methodological expectations for the field.

**Methods/skills:** designing moderation-of-process studies; bootstrapped indirect effects (PROCESS); power analysis; pre-registration and the open-science workflow; reading a paper for p-hacking risk.

**Central debate(s):** Statistical mediation vs. experimental causal-chain evidence — which licenses process claims? How should the field weigh significance, effect size, and replicability post-2011/2015?

### Week 14 — The Frontier: Consumers, AI, Algorithms, and Automated Agents

**Sub-topics:** algorithm aversion/appreciation; consumer responses to AI and humanoid robots; uniqueness neglect and task objectivity; the changing locus of "decision."

**Key readings**

- `@longoni2019medicalai` **[R]** — Longoni, Chiara, Andrea Bonezzi, and Carey K. Morewedge (2019), "Resistance to Medical Artificial Intelligence," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 46 (4), 629–650. DOI: `10.1093/jcr/ucz013`. *Why canonical (frontier):* identifies *uniqueness neglect* as the driver of consumers' reluctance to use AI in high-stakes (health) decisions — the reference paper for AI resistance in consumer research.
- `@castelo2019algorithm` **[R]** — Castelo, Noah, Maarten W. Bos, and Donald R. Lehmann (2019), "Task-Dependent Algorithm Aversion," *Journal of Marketing Research*, 56 (5), 809–825. DOI: `10.1177/0022243719851788`. *Why canonical (frontier):* shows algorithm aversion is *task-dependent* (stronger for subjective tasks) and that perceived task objectivity is malleable — a foundational moderator paper.
- `@mende2019robots` **[R]** — Mende, Martin, Maura L. Scott, Jenny van Doorn, Dhruv Grewal, and Ilana Shanks (2019), "Service Robots Rising: How Humanoid Robots Influence Service Experiences and Elicit Compensatory Consumer Responses," *Journal of Marketing Research*, 56 (4), 535–556. DOI: `10.1177/0022243718822827`. *Why canonical (frontier):* links humanoid service robots to discomfort and *compensatory* consumption, connecting the AI frontier back to the self/identity core of the seminar.
- `@karmarkar2015cost` **[R-method bridge]** — Karmarkar, Uma R., Baba Shiv, and Brian Knutson (2015), "Cost Conscious? The Neural and Behavioral Impact of Price Primacy on Decision Making," *Journal of Marketing Research*, 52 (4), 467–481. DOI: `10.1509/jmr.13.0488`. *Why canonical (frontier method):* a neuromarketing exemplar showing how information sequencing reshapes the neural computation of value — a window onto process-level frontier methods.

**Methods/skills:** designing human-vs-algorithm comparison studies; measuring trust/adoption; integrating process measures (including neural/physiological) with choice; thinking about generative-AI agents as consumers and as decision aids.

**Central debate(s):** Do consumers categorically avoid algorithms, or is aversion task- and framing-contingent (and sometimes *appreciation*)? As AI agents transact on consumers' behalf, what is the unit of "consumer behavior"?

## 4. How this chapter should expand over time

The skeleton above is stable, but the *frontier* modules (Weeks 12–14 and the swap-ins) are where the chapter should grow. Concretely:

1. **Generative-AI as decision agent and as "consumer."** The 2019 consumer–AI papers (Longoni; Castelo; Mende) predate large language models. As verified version-of-record work accrues in JCR/JMR/JCP/Marketing Science on (a) consumers' trust in and delegation to LLM agents, (b) AI as a *co-creator* of consumption experiences, and (c) "synthetic respondents"/LLMs as substitutes or supplements for human subjects, add a dedicated module and retire `@karmarkar2015cost` to a methods footnote.

2. **Privacy, data, and algorithmic personalization from the consumer's side.** A module on perceived surveillance, the privacy paradox, personalization–intrusiveness tradeoffs, and creepiness — pairing behavioral CB work with the privacy/personalization strand already catalogued in the quantitative chapters.

3. **The post-replication methods canon, kept current.** As multi-lab replications, Registered Reports, many-analysts studies, and meta-analytic re-evaluations of marquee effects (ego depletion, prosocial spending, choice overload) reach version-of-record, fold the strongest into Week 13 so students debate *specific* contested effects, not just the abstract crisis.

4. **Affective and physiological frontier.** Expand the affect module with verified work on emotional AI / affective computing, interoception, and consumer neuroscience as the top-4 evidence base matures.

5. **Transformative consumer research and well-being.** Sustainability (SHIFT), financial well-being, scarcity, dignity, and access/sharing are converging into a coherent well-being track; as more JM/JCR generalizing reviews land, promote the Week-12 well-being material into its own module.

6. **Identity in a platform/creator world.** Update the identity and social-influence modules with verified work on online identity, self-presentation, parasocial/creator relationships, and digital possessions (the "Liquid Consumption" line is the seed).

**Curation rule going forward (consistent with the rest of this research set):** add a reading only after its DOI is Crossref-verified to a published version-of-record in a top behavioral outlet (JCR, JCP, JMR, JM, Marketing Science, or the flagship psychology journals these literatures draw on); prefer review/meta-analytic anchors for new modules; and when a classic effect is challenged, cite *both* the original and the verified replication/meta-analysis so the debate is taught honestly.
