### 1. What drives virtual influencers' impact?

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Giovanni Luca Cascio Rizzo, Francisco Villarroel Ordenes, Jonah Berger — *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* (online first, 2026) — [10.1007/s11747-026-01185-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-026-01185-x)
- **Why it matters:** The book has **no** occurrence of "virtual influencer" anywhere in 70 chapters, and this is the flagship result on them. Combining automated image and text analysis of thousands of posts with controlled experiments, the authors show that *social tie presence* — the virtual influencer appearing in photos interacting with humans, not merely co-present with them — raises engagement, via perceived humanness and then trust. The effect is stronger when the influencer looks *less* humanlike, and attenuates when the companion is merely present, when the companion is itself virtual, and when the poster is a real human. That last boundary condition is what makes it teachable: it is a mechanism that exists *only* for synthetic personas.
- **Home:** `15-influencer-marketing.qmd` — new subsection under `## Drivers of Influencer Effectiveness {#sec-effectiveness-drivers}` (line 150), sibling to `### Sponsored Blogging`. Second home: `34-image-processing.qmd` — `## Three Families of Marketing Image Features` (line 325) or `## Applications in Advertising and Social Media` (line 538).
- **What to write:** In `15-influencer-marketing.qmd`, a subsection "The Virtual Influencer" arguing that the person-brand logic in `### The Influencer as a Person Brand` (line 68) does not transfer intact to computer-generated personas: authenticity cannot be assumed from a body, so it must be *signalled*, and depicted social ties are the cheapest available signal. State the humanness → trust → engagement chain and the reversal by influencer realism (less humanlike agents gain more from social ties). Note the managerial implication for the reach–cost trade-off in `### Selecting Influencers Under a Reach–Cost Trade-off` (line 399): a virtual account's engagement is partly a *content design* variable, not a fixed account attribute, which breaks the account-level multiples logic in `## Valuing Influencer Accounts and Posts` (line 502). In `34-image-processing.qmd`, use it as the worked case for face-detection-derived features: the authors operationalize companion presence via the count of detected faces per image (Google Cloud Vision face detection, 100% accuracy on a 100-image validation sample), which is exactly the "generated feature as regressor" problem the chapter raises at `## The Econometrics of Generated Image Features {#sec-image-econometrics}` (line 443) — a cheap, validated feature, with the validation sample reported.
- **Replication:** No public package confirmed. Open preprint: <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.09874>; SSRN working paper 4329150.

### 2. A meta-analysis of green marketing communication effectiveness

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Mojtaba (Moji) Barari, Alicia Kulczynski, Martin Eisend — *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* (online first, 2026) — [10.1007/s11747-026-01189-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-026-01189-7)
- **Why it matters:** 156 studies, 146,961 participants. The headline is a genuine reversal of the intuitive prediction: green marketing communication (GMC) is *less* effective for green brands than for conventional brands. The moderator structure is prescriptive and splits by brand type — for green brands, GMC works better when the brand is new, and when the message is emotional, abstract, package-delivered, and backed by *lower* levels of evidence; conventional brands get a different profile. That "lower evidence works better for green brands" finding is a teachable tension with the disclosure/substantiation logic already in the TCR seminar.
- **Home:** `65-public-policy-tcr-seminar.qmd` — `## Week 8 — Sustainability and pro-environmental behavior {#sec-tcr-week08}` (line 314). Second home: `10-advertising.qmd`, in the message-content / meta-analytic evidence discussion.
- **What to write:** In Week 8, add it as the quantitative anchor for the sustainability reading list and write the paradox out: firms with the strongest environmental credentials get the *smallest* communication return, because a green brand's positioning is already priced into consumer priors, so GMC is redundant for them and diagnostic for everyone else. Then set up the policy tension against `## Week 3 — Information, disclosure, and deception` (line 170): the finding that lower evidence levels outperform for green brands is precisely the pattern a substantiation regime is designed to suppress, so effectiveness and compliance pull in opposite directions here. In `10-advertising.qmd`, cite it as the current best effect-size estimate for green appeals and as an example of a meta-analysis whose value is the moderator table rather than the grand mean.
- **Replication:** No public package confirmed. Meta-analytic coding sheets are sometimes posted as JAMS supplementary material — worth a check at integration time before writing "none."

### 3. Does TikTok promote or cannibalize music streaming? Estimands and identification with heavy-tailed outcomes

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Daniel Winkler, Christian Hotz-Behofsits, Nils Wlömert, Dominik Papies, Jūra Liaukonytė — *Quantitative Marketing and Economics* 24(1), 2026 — [10.1007/s11129-026-09312-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11129-026-09312-2)
- **Why it matters:** The strongest methodological keeper of the week, and it resolves a live empirical dispute. Several teams used the same quasi-experiment — Universal Music Group pulling its catalog from TikTok globally — and reached opposite conclusions. The authors show the disagreement is not about identification but about **estimand**: with heavy-tailed outcomes, DiD in levels, in logs, and in Poisson weight different parts of the outcome distribution and therefore answer different economic questions. In their data the top 10% of songs account for 96% of TikTok creations and 76% of the effect mass; removing TikTok access lowers Spotify demand for UMG titles, with the loss concentrated in viral titles and no economically meaningful movement in the long tail. Levels and logs are not two estimators of one parameter — they are two parameters.
- **Home:** `47-causal-inference.qmd` — `## Difference-in-Differences {#sec-causal-did}` (line 893), and as the assigned reading in `### Week 5 — Difference-in-Differences I` (line 221) or `### Week 6 — Difference-in-Differences II` (line 255). Second home: `06-virality.qmd` (or `51-platforms.qmd`) for the substantive platform-spillover result.
- **What to write:** A subsection in `## Difference-in-Differences` — "Which average? Estimands under heavy-tailed outcomes" — making the point that the functional-form choice in a DiD *is* an estimand choice, not a robustness check. A level-DiD reports an average treatment effect in units and is dominated by the right tail; a log-DiD reports something closer to a median proportional effect and is dominated by the mass of small units (and is undefined at zero); Poisson/PPML reports a proportional effect weighted by baseline size. Marketing outcomes — streams, views, sales, impressions — are almost all heavy-tailed, so this is the default case, not the exception. Prescribe the discipline the paper implies: state the estimand in words before choosing the link function, and report the tail decomposition (what share of the effect sits in the top decile) alongside the point estimate. This slots naturally beside the existing TWFE-critique material and strengthens `## Threats Common to All Designs {#sec-causal-threats}` (line 1352). Cross-reference from `06-virality.qmd`: virality metrics are the canonical heavy-tailed outcome, and the substantive result — that promotion effects concentrate in already-viral titles — is itself a statement about superstar dynamics rather than a general platform-complementarity finding.
- **Replication:** No public package confirmed — I checked the arXiv listing and no code/data repository is linked. Open preprint: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14999>; SSRN working paper 6143552.

### 4. Firms' Focus on Brand and Customer Management: Measurement, Development, and Financial Consequences

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Thomas P. Scholdra, Simeng Han, Werner J. Reinartz, Bernd Skiera — *Journal of Marketing* (online first, 2026) — [10.1177/00222429261478881](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429261478881)
- **Why it matters:** Settles a long-running strategic-marketing debate by measuring the thing the debate assumed. Using automated text analysis of 95,000+ earnings conference call transcripts from 1,800+ firms over 22 years, plus manual coding and secondary data, the authors show brand-management focus and customer-management focus are **uncorrelated** — there is neither a systematic trade-off nor a systematic complementarity between them. That kills the framing of the debate as "which one matters more" and replaces it with two separable strategic dials, each with its own financial consequences. It is also a clean example of constructing a firm-level marketing-strategy measure from a text corpus, which the book teaches nowhere.
- **Home:** `marketing-strategy.qmd` — `## Marketing Organization, the CMO, and Accountability {#sec-ms-organization}` (line 451), and/or `## The Marketing-Finance Interface: The Accountability Turn {#sec-ms-finance}` (line 365). Second home: `49-text-as-data.qmd` — `## Marketing Applications {#sec-tad-applications}` (line 575). Third, optional: `21-marketing-finance.qmd` — `## Marketing, Information, and the Stock Market` (line 568).
- **What to write:** In `marketing-strategy.qmd`, a paragraph stating that brand focus and customer focus are empirically orthogonal at the firm level, so treating them as endpoints of a single strategic-emphasis continuum is a measurement error rather than a modeling simplification — firms can be high on both, low on both, or specialized, and the four cells have different performance profiles. Tie it to the accountability discussion: what a CMO says on an earnings call is a disclosed, dated, machine-readable record of strategic emphasis, which makes emphasis a *panel* variable rather than a cross-sectional survey item. In `49-text-as-data.qmd`, use it as the worked application for corpus-based firm-level construct measurement — the design pattern is dictionary/classifier over a long-horizon disclosure corpus, validated against manual coding on a subsample, then merged to financial panels. ### 5. Channel Adoption Pathways and Post-Adoption Behavior

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Shirsho Biswas, Hema Yoganarasimhan, Haonan Zhang — *Journal of Marketing* (online first, 2026) — [10.1177/00222429261478875](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429261478875)
- **Why it matters:** The multichannel literature's standing result is that multichannel customers are more valuable. This paper shows that result is conditional on *why* the customer became multichannel. Using transaction-level panel data from a large Brazilian pet-supplies retailer and DiD estimates, the authors follow offline-only customers who adopt online through four distinct pathways — organic, COVID-19, Black Friday promotion, and loyalty program — and find the pathway is associated with materially different post-adoption spending and profitability. It is a direct correction to a claim the book's CLV logic implicitly relies on, and a textbook illustration of selection-into-treatment: the "treatment" (channel adoption) is the same, the counterfactual is not.
- **Home:** `13-clv.qmd` — `## Causal and Decision-Focused CLV {#sec-clv-causal}` (line 937), with a cross-reference from `## Identification and What Breaks It {#sec-clv-identification}` (line 1204). Second home: `63-channels-b2b-seminar.qmd`, which already carries the book's multichannel material (8 occurrences).
- **What to write:** In `13-clv.qmd`, a paragraph warning that the multichannel-customers-are-more-valuable regularity is an *average over adoption pathways*, and that the pathway is a selection mechanism, not a covariate to be controlled away. A promotion-induced adopter and an organically-induced adopter can post identical first-year channel usage and diverge sharply in profitability, so a CLV model that conditions on observed channel breadth without conditioning on how breadth was acquired will systematically over-value promotion-acquired multichannel customers. Draw the managerial inference the paper supports: the ROI of a channel-migration campaign depends on which pathway it imitates, so "drive customers online" is not a well-defined objective. In `63-channels-b2b-seminar.qmd`, add it to the multichannel reading with the pathway taxonomy (organic / shock / promotion / loyalty program) as the discussion frame.

### 6. The Moment of Delivery in Online Retailing: How Courier Types and Inoculation Messages Affect Reactions to the Last-Mile Experience

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Christopher Funke, Gianfranco Walsh, Tobias Schaefers — *Journal of Service Research* (online first, 2026) — [10.1177/10946705261464334](https://doi.org/10.1177/10946705261464334)
- **Why it matters:** "Last-mile" appears exactly **once** in the entire book (in `56-geospatial.qmd`), which is a conspicuous hole for a research book that covers service failure and recovery in depth. The moment of delivery is the *only* interpersonal touchpoint in most online journeys, and it is routinely outsourced. An analysis of 35,000+ reviews shows negative delivery experiences produce ripple effects through negative WOM about the *retailer*; four experiments then compare two remedies — vertical integration (proprietary couriers) and inoculation messaging. The finding that a communication remedy can substitute for an ownership remedy is a genuinely useful managerial result and a nice bridge between the service-recovery and channel-governance literatures.

- **What to write:** In `18-service.qmd`, extend the recovery material to the case where the failing agent is not the firm's employee. State that attribution does not follow ownership: consumers blame the retailer for a third-party courier's failure, so outsourcing the touchpoint outsources the operation but not the reputational liability. Then present the two remedies as an explicit make-or-message choice — vertical integration buys control at fixed cost, inoculation (pre-emptively warning the customer that delivery is handled by a partner and that problems will be resolved by the retailer) buys partial protection at near-zero marginal cost — and note that this is *pre*-failure recovery, which the chapter's current recovery-paradox framing does not cover. ### 7. Make it Stick: The Role of Alternative Activities in Reducing Smartphone Usage

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Quang Duc Pham, Jeeva Somasundaram, Laura Zimmermann — *Journal of Public Policy & Marketing* (online first, 2026) — [10.1177/07439156261476237](https://doi.org/10.1177/07439156261476237)
- **Why it matters:** Two longitudinal field studies, 153 individuals, 5,686 observations of **objectively measured** smartphone use — not self-report. The design question is the one that matters for every incentivized behavior-change intervention: what happens after the incentive stops? Participants incentivized both to cut usage *and* to take up a positive alternative (learning a language, walking) sustain the reduction post-incentive better than those incentivized only to cut usage. That is a substitution account of habit change, and it generalizes well past smartphones.
- **Home:** `16-nudges.qmd` — the persistence/decay discussion. Second home: `60-field-experiments-seminar.qmd` (long-run and post-intervention measurement) and/or `65-public-policy-tcr-seminar.qmd` — `## Week 13 — Dark patterns, technology harms, and addiction {#sec-tcr-week13}` (line 466).
- **What to write:** In `16-nudges.qmd`, the point is that a nudge or incentive that only *subtracts* a behavior leaves a vacuum that the original habit refills, so persistence requires supplying a replacement rather than raising the cost of the target behavior. Use it to sharpen the standing distinction between effects measured during an intervention and effects measured after it — the paper's design (incentive period followed by a post-incentive observation window) is the minimum credible design for any persistence claim, and most published nudge effects do not have it. In `60-field-experiments-seminar.qmd`, hold it up as a methods exemplar: objectively logged behavioral outcomes, high-frequency longitudinal observation, and a pre-registered post-treatment window — the combination that makes decay estimable rather than assumed. In the TCR seminar, add to Week 13's technology-harms reading.

### 8. The Diminishing Value of Bundling Under Inventory Scarcity

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Tarek Abdallah, Josh Reed — *Management Science* (online first, 2026) — [10.1287/mnsc.2025.03120](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.03120)
- **Why it matters:** A clean boundary condition on a result the book currently teaches without qualification. Classical bundling theory — Adams–Yellen, Schmalensee, Bakos–Brynjolfsson — assumes abundant inventory and static prices, and concludes bundling raises revenue by reducing valuation heterogeneity. The authors show that limited inventory distorts that revenue-extraction mechanism and can favor selling separately. They characterize optimal dynamic mixed bundling in a large-market regime (market size growing relative to fixed inventory) and derive the optimal dynamic pricing policy. The book's bundling material sits in `17-pricing.qmd` (8 occurrences) and `26-analytical-models.qmd` (`## Branding, Bundling, and Mixed Strategies`, line 1654) with no scarcity qualifier at all.
- **Home:** `17-pricing.qmd` — `### Second-Degree Discrimination: Self-Selection` (line 285), with a cross-reference from `### Dynamic Pricing: Learning and Inventory` (line 678). Second home: `26-analytical-models.qmd` — `## Branding, Bundling, and Mixed Strategies {#sec-branding-bundling}` (line 1654), and the `39-analytical-modeling-seminar.qmd` bundling reading.
- **What to write:** A qualifying paragraph: the classical bundling advantage is an *unconstrained-supply* result. Bundling extracts surplus by aggregating valuations, but it also commits units of both products to a single transaction; when inventory is scarce relative to demand, that commitment forecloses the option to allocate each unit to its highest-value buyer, and the option value of separate sale can exceed the aggregation gain. Concretely, this is the intersection of two things the pricing chapter already teaches separately — second-degree discrimination via self-selection, and dynamic pricing under inventory constraints — and the paper's contribution is that they interact rather than compose. Note the practical scope: services and events with hard capacity (airlines, hotels, ticketing, limited-edition goods) are exactly where the textbook bundling prescription is most likely to be wrong.

### 11. Finding Important Interdependent Product Features: A Simultaneous Consideration of Price and Quality Features

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Sangkil Moon, Eunhee (Emily) Ko, Jong Min Kim, Keeyeon Ki-cheon Park — *Journal of Interactive Marketing* (online first, 2026) — [10.1177/10949968261478847](https://doi.org/10.1177/10949968261478847)
- **Why it matters:** A specific, teachable methodological hook the text chapter is missing. Review-based feature importance is a workhorse technique, and it has a structural defect nobody states clearly: reviewers mention only a handful of features, so the review–feature matrix is mostly missing, and the missingness is **not** random — people write about what struck them. The authors treat this explicitly as a missing-data problem, test multiple imputation methods, select empirically, and only then estimate feature importance, including interdependence between price and quality features (e.g. cleanliness and sleep quality jointly with value-for-money in hotels). `49-text-as-data.qmd` has an aspect-based sentiment section and a pitfalls section but never names the missingness problem; `missing data`/`imputation` appear nowhere in that chapter.
- **Home:** `49-text-as-data.qmd` — `### Aspect-Based Sentiment {#sec-aspect-sentiment}` (line 436), with a cross-reference from `## Pitfalls and Identification {#sec-tad-pitfalls}` (line 613). Second home: `32-preference-measurement.qmd`, contrasting revealed feature importance from reviews with stated importance from conjoint.
- **What to write:** In `49-text-as-data.qmd`, add the warning explicitly: a review corpus does not give you a rectangular feature matrix, it gives you a sparse one, and treating unmentioned as unimportant (or as neutral, or as zero) embeds an MNAR assumption in what looks like a preprocessing decision. Silence is informative — a feature may go unmentioned because it was unremarkable *or* because it was irrelevant to that reviewer, and those imply opposite importance weights. State the paper's remedy — impute the missing cells, choose the imputation method by empirical comparison rather than convention, then estimate importance on the completed matrix, allowing price and quality features to be interdependent rather than separately ranked. In `32-preference-measurement.qmd`, use it to make the comparison sharp: conjoint asks about every attribute by construction, reviews do not, so review-derived importance and conjoint-derived importance are not measuring the same quantity even when they rank features identically.

### 12. When Consumer Financial Protection Spills Over: Student Loan Borrowing Under the CARD Act

- **Authors / Journal / DOI:** Alexander L. Brown, Daniel Grodzicki, Paolina C. Medina — *Management Science* (online first, 2026) — [10.1287/mnsc.2024.06339](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06339)
- **Why it matters:** This is a *marketing* regulation, not merely a finance one: CARD Act Title 3 §304 specifically restricted the marketing and sale of credit cards on college campuses. Using a DiD that treats incoming freshmen who would not have been exposed to the campus marketing as controls, the authors document a spillover — student loan borrowing rose 8.4%, and 14% among less affluent students — with survey evidence, matched to administrative records, supporting substitutability between the two credit sources. The regressive gradient is the part worth teaching: the intervention protected everyone and cost the least affluent the most. The TCR seminar's Week 12 on financial decision-making and consumer protection needs exactly this kind of case, and the book currently has no worked example of a marketing restriction producing a substitution externality.
- **Home:** `65-public-policy-tcr-seminar.qmd` — `## Week 12 — Financial decision-making and consumer protection {#sec-tcr-week12}` (line 435). Second home: `47-causal-inference.qmd` — `### Week 5 — Difference-in-Differences I` (line 221) or `## Difference-in-Differences {#sec-causal-did}` (line 893), as a cohort-based DiD where the control group is defined by *timing of exposure to marketing* rather than by geography.
- **What to write:** In the TCR seminar Week 12, use it as the canonical unintended-consequence case: restricting a marketing channel does not remove the underlying demand, it redirects it, and the redirect can be worse for the consumer than the thing that was banned — student debt is harder to discharge than credit-card debt. Emphasize that the welfare evaluation of a marketing restriction must be conducted over the full substitution set, not over the restricted product alone, and that the distributional gradient (14% for less affluent students against 8.4% overall) means an average-effect evaluation would understate the harm to the intended beneficiaries. In `47-causal-inference.qmd`, the design is the teachable part: the control group is an adjacent *cohort* — freshmen arriving after campus marketing had stopped — which is a nice illustration of constructing a comparison group from the timing of exposure when no untreated geography exists, along with the parallel-trends assumption that requires.
