75  Writing the Policy-and-Welfare Paper for Marketing Journals

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The preceding three chapters assembled a research architecture: a causal estimate of a spillover into an untargeted market, a normative model that says what optimal behavior looks like, a calibrated welfare bound, and an independent complementary test. The architecture is field-neutral. Whether a paper built on it lands in Management Science, Marketing Science, the Journal of Marketing, the Journal of Marketing Research, the Journal of Consumer Research, or the Journal of Public Policy & Marketing depends on choices about framing, about which component carries the contribution, and about whose decision problem the paper is written for.

This chapter is about those choices. It maps the architecture onto the marketing journals, sets out the five-move template that these papers follow, translates “consumer financial protection” into the marketing-instrument vocabulary that makes the question ours rather than borrowed, supplies a portfolio of concrete project designs, and closes with the objections reviewers raise and how to pre-empt them.

75.1 Why This Paper Type Belongs in Marketing

There is a reflex to treat consumer-protection research as economics that happens to involve consumers. The reflex is wrong for a specific reason: almost every consequential consumer regulation of the past two decades restricts a marketing instrument. Section 304 of the CARD Act did not cap interest rates or limit credit lines; it banned marketing—the tables, the giveaways, the on-campus promotion of a financial product to a young audience (Brown, Grodzicki, and Medina 2026). Advertising bans restrict advertising (Dubois, Griffith, and O’Connell 2018). Privacy regimes restrict targeting and data joins (Goldfarb and Tucker 2011; Johnson, Shriver, and Goldberg 2023; Aridor, Che, and Salz 2023). Do-not-call and anti-spam rules restrict channels (Goh, Hui, and Png 2011). Disclosure mandates restrict message content. Dark-pattern rules restrict interface design.

Once a rule is understood as a constraint on the marketing mix, the questions it raises are marketing questions. What is the cross-elasticity between the restricted instrument and its substitutes? Which segments were reachable only through the restricted instrument? How does the firm reallocate budget, and what does the reallocation do to the consumers who were the rule’s intended beneficiaries? Marketing owns the substitution structure of consumer choice, the measurement of instrument effectiveness, and the models of how firms allocate across instruments. That is a comparative advantage, and it is the argument the introduction of such a paper should make in its second paragraph.

The field’s editorial statements have been explicit that work bearing on consumer and societal welfare is wanted, not tolerated (Chandy et al. 2021; Moorman et al. 2019), and the transformative-consumer-research tradition supplies a well-developed vocabulary for well-being outcomes (Mick et al. 2012; Wilkie and Moore 1999). What has been scarcer is work that combines that orientation with the identification and modeling standards of the quantitative track. The architecture in these chapters is one way to supply it.

75.2 The Journal-Fit Map

The same underlying study can be written five ways. Table 75.1 sets out what each outlet wants as the load-bearing contribution, what role the model plays, and what the review process will attack hardest.

Two implications are worth stating plainly. First, the Marketing Science version and the Journal of Marketing version are not the same paper with different abstracts—they have different section orders, different figure sets, and different robustness sections. Deciding the outlet after the analysis is finished is a common and expensive mistake. Second, the welfare object is a positioning choice, not merely a technical one: a paper whose welfare object is firm profit is a strategy paper, and one whose object is consumer surplus is a policy paper, and reviewers evaluate them against different literatures.

75.3 The Five-Move Template

Papers with this architecture follow a recognizable sequence, shown in Figure 75.1. Each move answers the objection raised by the previous one, which is why the order is difficult to rearrange.

Move 1 — the institutional shock. Establish that a specific instrument became more costly on a specific date, with enough institutional detail that a reader can see who was exposed and who was not. This section is longer than authors expect and is where the identification is actually won.

Move 2 — the spillover. Estimate the effect on the untargeted market with a design whose control group is defined by exposure rather than geography (Chapter 71). Lead with the event study, not the pooled coefficient. Include the theory-predicted heterogeneity as a specification test.

Move 3 — the normative model. Write down the small model, derive the threshold, and state which departures are possible (Chapter 72). Two to four pages. Resist estimating it.

Move 4 — mechanism. Measure which departures are present and in what proportion, ideally with a record-linked survey (Chapter 74). This section supplies the population shares the welfare calculation needs, so it must come before the welfare section.

Move 5 — welfare and corroboration. Calibrate the bound (Chapter 73), report it by subgroup with a sensitivity analysis, and then present an independent downstream test whose failure modes differ.

Anatomy of the abstract

The anchor paper’s abstract is a compact instance of the template and repays imitation. It names the provision and what it did; concedes that the intended effect occurred (“while it reduced card use”); states the spillover with a magnitude and a distributional split; explains that a survey was designed to assess the substitution and what it revealed; states that model-based evidence implies a welfare gain; and closes with the complementary analysis of academic outcomes. Six sentences, five moves, one concession. The concession is doing strategic work: acknowledging the intended effect first buys the credibility to report the unintended one.

75.4 Generalizing the Template: Analysis and Assessment

The five moves above are a template for one paper type: a regulatory restriction with an untargeted substitute market and a price ratio to anchor a normative cutoff. Most initiatives a marketing scholar would want to evaluate are not shaped that way. A brand’s supplier-diversity commitment, a retailer’s bag charge, a platform’s safety re-ranking, a firm’s decision to fund a community program instead of cutting prices— each has social consequences and none has a posted price ratio. Kaul, Luo, and Singh (2026) supply the more general scaffold, and it is worth reading alongside the template because it shows which of the five moves is doing general work and which is an artifact of the anchor paper’s setting.

Their starting definition is the one Section 44.2 already uses, relocated from a treatment to an organizational initiative: impact is the difference between the states of the world with and without the initiative. Everything follows from taking that counterfactual seriously. The framework then splits into a descriptive step and a normative one, and the split is the source of most of its discipline, because the two steps fail in different ways and are usually conflated.

The analysis step is descriptive and has three legs. Goal realization asks how far the initiative achieved the social goal it stated—the question a program evaluation usually stops at. Spillovers ask what else it moved, financially or societally, which is the same object Chapter 71 is built around, arrived at from a different literature and given the same name. Alternative initiatives ask how the initiative performed relative to other ways of organizing to achieve the same goal.

That third leg is the one the five-move template does not have, and it is the more demanding bar. Move 2 asks whether behavior stopped or relocated; it compares the policy to no policy. Kaul and coauthors ask what else the organization could have done with the same resources, which is an opportunity-cost counterfactual. A finding that a loyalty-funded literacy program raised literacy is a Move-2 finding. A finding that it raised literacy less per dollar than a price cut to the same households would have is an analysis-step finding, and it is the one a manager can act on. Marketing is unusually well placed to supply it, because a firm’s alternative uses of the same budget are typically marketing instruments whose effects the field already knows how to estimate.

The assessment step is normative, and its contribution is to name three routes rather than one. Pareto improvement asks whether anyone was made worse off—the cleanest standard, and rarely satisfied, since almost every marketing intervention takes from someone. Net impact aggregates benefits and costs into a single metric, which is what the welfare bound of Chapter 73 does; it buys comparability by committing to an exchange rate between outcomes, and that commitment is the assumption reviewers should attack. Trade-off evaluation declines the exchange rate and characterizes the trade-offs instead, along four dimensions: relative magnitude, equity (who bears the cost), certainty (how confidently each side is known), and agency (whether those affected chose the exposure).

Figure 75.2 lays the scaffold out.

The agency dimension is the one marketing should claim. Whether the people bearing a cost chose their exposure to it is precisely the question Chapter 20 is about, and it is what separates a default from a ban, a disclosed sponsorship from an undisclosed one, a price increase from a dark pattern. A welfare number that aggregates across that distinction has thrown away the variable the field knows most about.

Writing the paper in this mould

The framework is a research agenda, and the agenda is written for a management audience; the marketing version is not yet written. A tractable project takes one initiative and does the analysis step completely, which almost nobody does.

  1. Name the initiative and its counterfactual explicitly, in the with-and-without form. Most papers name the initiative and leave the counterfactual implicit, which is where the disagreements later turn out to live.
  2. Estimate goal realization with the field’s own designs. This is a standard causal-inference exercise (Chapter 44) and should be the shortest part.
  3. Search for spillovers where theory says substitution goes, using the taxonomy in Section 71.2. Report the untargeted-market effect with the same care as the targeted one.
  4. Construct at least one alternative initiative and price it. The credible ones are the firm’s own foregone options: the same budget spent on price, on the salesforce, on the product. This is the move that makes it a marketing paper rather than a program evaluation.
  5. Choose the assessment route in the paper, and defend the choice. Test Pareto first because it is cheap and occasionally passes. If it fails, either commit to a metric and defend the exchange rate, or report the four trade-off dimensions and decline to aggregate. Declining to aggregate is a legitimate result, provided the dimensions are measured rather than gestured at.
  6. Triangulate the normative claim the way Chapter 74 prescribes, since a conclusion that survives two assessment routes is worth far more than one that survives a longer robustness table under a single route.

The reviewer objection to anticipate is that the alternative-initiative comparison is unidentified, because the firm did not run the alternative. It is a fair objection and the answer is a bound: pick alternatives whose effects the literature has already pinned down with meta-analytic precision—advertising elasticities, price elasticities, salesforce response—and report the comparison as an interval. A bounded opportunity cost is more informative than a precisely estimated goal-realization effect with no comparison at all.

75.6 A Portfolio of Project Designs

What follows are settings in which the full architecture is feasible with data that exists. Each entry names the restricted instrument, the untargeted market where the substitution should appear, the identification handle, the normative benchmark, and the natural outlet. They are starting points to be sharpened, not finished designs.

Row 10 has a precedent outside the policy literature. Kim et al. (2026) study on-demand wage access, an employer-linked benefit that lets low-wage workers draw earned pay before the pay date, as an alternative to costly short-term credit such as payday loans. Using transaction records from about 4,000 users of one U.S. platform and a staggered-adoption difference-in-differences design, they find that adoption raises monthly saving frequency by 3.7 percent and time spent monitoring financial dashboards by 12.9 percent, with larger effects where minimum wages are lower or banking access is thinner. The design observes the same units before and after the new instrument arrives, which is the binding constraint named above. It does not observe the credit products the platform may displace, so the substitution leg of the architecture remains open.

Screening candidate projects before you invest

A project is viable only if all five of the following hold. (1) The shock has a date and a scope you can document from primary sources. (2) The substitute market is observed for the same units. (3) There is exposure variation independent of the shock date. (4) The two instruments have posted or recoverable prices, since the normative cutoff is a price ratio. (5) A downstream outcome exists that theory says should move. Failing (2) or (3) kills the project; failing (4) downgrades it from a welfare paper to a substitution paper, which is still publishable but in a different place.

75.6.1 Sizing the design before collecting data

Spillover effects are second-order by construction—a fraction of an already modest first-order effect—so power is a real constraint rather than a formality. The calculation below gives the minimum detectable effect for a two-group panel design of the kind used throughout these chapters, as a function of the number of units and the within-unit correlation of the outcome.

Code
library(ggplot2)

mde <- function(n_units, n_periods, rho = 0.6, p_treat = 0.5,
                power = 0.80, alpha = 0.05) {
  # Design effect for repeated measures on the same unit.
  deff <- 1 + (n_periods - 1) * rho
  n_eff <- n_units * n_periods / deff
  (qnorm(1 - alpha / 2) + qnorm(power)) *
    sqrt(1 / (n_eff * p_treat * (1 - p_treat)))
}

pw <- expand.grid(n_units = c(2000, 5000, 20000, 70000),
                  n_periods = 2:12, rho = c(0.3, 0.7))
pw$mde <- mapply(mde, pw$n_units, pw$n_periods, pw$rho)

ggplot(pw, aes(n_periods, mde, colour = factor(n_units))) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 0.8) +
  facet_wrap(~ paste("Within-unit correlation =", rho)) +
  scale_colour_discrete(name = "Units") +
  labs(x = "Periods observed per unit",
       y = "Minimum detectable effect (SD units)",
       title = "Power for a spillover panel design") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 11)

The practical reading: with the roughly 70,000 students and eight semesters of the anchor study, effects well under a tenth of a standard deviation are detectable, which is why an 8 percent movement in balances could be estimated precisely enough to support subgroup analysis. A study with two thousand units and three periods is not going to find a spillover unless it is enormous, and should be redesigned rather than run.

75.7 A Second Paper Type: The Randomized Campaign Evaluation

The architecture of the preceding chapters is one way to write a policy paper. There is a simpler type that uses almost no theory. It takes a marketing program that someone actually ran, randomizes it at scale, and measures the outcomes the program exists to change. It belongs in this chapter because its contribution is still a policy contribution. It is also the type a doctoral student can most realistically execute, because it needs a partner and a design rather than a model. Donati et al. (2026a), published in Marketing Science, is a clean recent instance. Their question is the title of the paper: can Facebook ads prevent malaria? They answer it with two field experiments, administrative health records, and a cost-per-case calculation.

Which version the numbers come from

The figures in this section come from the 2024 World Bank working paper (Donati et al. 2024). Its tables are open, and the World Bank’s reproducibility package for it holds the data and the authors’ code. Every estimate quoted here from Tables 2 to 5, 7, and D17 was reproduced from that package (Section 75.7.3). Only a one-line teaser abstract of the published article was accessible when this section was written. The 2026 CESifo version (Donati et al. 2026b) frames some results differently; those differences are noted where they matter. Check the published tables before quoting a number.

The program was a social-marketing campaign by the nonprofit Malaria No More, Bite Ko Mat Lo Lite (“don’t take the bite lightly”), funded by Facebook’s Campaigns for a Healthier World initiative. Its ads urged people to sleep under bed nets and to get tested within 24 hours of a fever. It ran on Facebook and Instagram from July 2020 to early January 2021 and spent $202,000. It reached about 130 million users in 22 states, at an average of 3.6 impressions each. It was optimized for engagement: the team switched off weaker ads and moved budget to the best-performing ones. Campaigns like this are usually judged by engagement and by platform brand-lift surveys, and by those measures this one succeeded. The paper asks two questions those measures cannot answer. Did the campaign change offline behavior and disease? And did it work for the people most at risk?

The design has five moves, shown in Figure 75.4.

  1. Randomize the real campaign, not a copy of it. The nonprofit agreed to delay launch in three high-burden states (Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, and Chhattisgarh) so that 40 of 80 districts could be randomized to receive it. One district was dropped because two districts shared a name on the randomization list, leaving 79 for analysis. The platform cannot target ads by district boundary, so the team drew circles around each district’s main cities and the advertiser excluded the control circles. Randomizing clusters rather than individuals matches how both the ads and the disease spread. It also makes every estimate an intent-to-treat effect of the campaign as it was actually delivered.
  2. Measure outcomes off the platform, in layers. The team recruited respondents with ads that paid mobile credit for completing chatbot surveys on Messenger. A longitudinal sample of 5,205 reported behavior in up to eight short surveys during the campaign. A separately recruited sample of 3,052 reported household malaria one to three months after it ended. Monthly records of tests and positive cases by subdistrict, split into urban and rural facilities, came from India’s Health Management Information System. Each layer covers a weakness of another. Surveys capture behavior but invite demand effects. Facility records cannot be talked into a result, but they are coarse and under-count cases.
  3. Fix a risk marker in advance and power both sides of it. Formative surveys found that dwelling type was the strongest household predictor of malaria. Respondents in solid brick or cement houses were 43 percent less likely to report a recent case than those in mud, tin, or straw dwellings. Organic recruitment reached mostly solid-house dwellers, so recruitment was stratified by district and dwelling type. Lookalike audiences seeded with non-solid dwellers brought about 55 percent of respondents from higher-risk housing. The marker also maps onto the facility data, because non-solid dwellings make up roughly 15 percent of urban and 50 percent of rural housing in India.
  4. Separate content from reach. A split by risk group cannot by itself say whether the ads failed to persuade the high-risk group or failed to reach it. A second experiment answers that question.
  5. Price the effect against alternatives. Convert the estimate into a cost per case averted and set it beside other ways of spending the same money.

The headline result is a split. Among respondents in solid houses, the campaign raised the share of survey waves in which they slept under a net by 5.5 percentage points (SE 2.5; control mean 60 percent). It raised help-seeking within 24 hours of a fever by 10.7 points (SE 3.5, among the 301 who reported one). It also cut self-reported household malaria after the campaign by 2.4 points (SE 1.1) on a control rate of 5.5 percent. Among respondents in non-solid houses, each of these estimates is small and insignificant. The facility records point the same way. Malaria cases recorded at urban facilities fell by 6.21 a month per million residents (SE 2.86), while cases at rural facilities did not detectably fall (−2.24, SE 5.79), and the overall estimate (−4.68, SE 5.37) cannot be distinguished from zero. The urban estimate is significant under the paper’s conventional test but not under a wild cluster bootstrap (p = 0.09; Section 75.7.3).

Was the problem content or reach? Ad recall at the end of the campaign rose 4.9 points among treated solid-house respondents but only 1.1 points (insignificant) among non-solid ones, which suggests the ads never arrived. The second experiment tested this directly. For two weeks, the team retargeted the campaign’s best ads, through a Custom Audience, to a random half of a fresh survey pool. They reached about 90 percent of that half, at 4.9 impressions each. With exposure equalized, bed-net use the previous night rose 7.5 points (SE 2.9) on a control rate of 65 percent. The rise was 7.6 points among solid dwellers and 8.1 among non-solid dwellers, and the estimated interaction was exactly zero (SE 5.7). So the ads worked for the high-risk group once they reached it. The engagement-optimized campaign mostly did not reach it, and the recruitment data show why in prices: recruiting a respondent in a non-solid house cost almost ten times as much.

That finding is the paper’s marketing contribution. Suppose a delivery algorithm is told to maximize engagement within a budget. It will spend on the cheapest responsive users, the mechanism Lambrecht and Tucker (2019) documented for job ads. In public health, the cheapest users are often the ones who need the message least. The cost calculation then completes the template. It multiplies the monthly effect by nine months and by the 1.1 billion people in the campaign’s states. That gives $3.35 per reported case averted using the urban estimate and $6.54 using the overall estimate. The paper sets these beside $5–6 per case averted for distributing insecticide-treated nets and $5.68 per additional COVID-19 vaccination from a meta-analysis of more than 800 Facebook public-health experiments (Athey et al. 2023).

75.7.1 Reading it as a referee would

A referee should look hard at four features of the paper. Each one is also a lesson for anyone writing in this mould.

The heterogeneity carries the policy content, so it needs the strongest possible status. In the 2024 version, the dwelling-type split leads the abstract. The 2026 CESifo abstract leads instead with small and insignificant average effects. It reframes the split in terms of socioeconomic status and calls it a post-hoc heterogeneity analysis. The 2024 working paper reports ethical approval but no trial-registry identifier (the published article’s front matter could not be checked). Compare Breza et al. (2021), who randomized U.S. counties to a Facebook campaign of doctors’ and nurses’ videos before the 2020 holidays. They list their trial registrations in the abstract, name three primary outcomes, and report each one: less holiday travel, no change in the share of people leaving home, and 3.5 percent fewer recorded infections. The malaria team chose its risk marker in formative research before the trial and stratified on it, so the subgroup analysis was built into the design; a registration would have let the paper say so. With many outcomes and two strata, a referee will also ask for a multiple-testing adjustment (List, Shaikh, and Xu 2019). The replication package makes one possible (Table 75.5). Across the six survey outcomes, three effects among solid-house dwellers survive at the 5 percent level: household bed-net use, help-seeking within 24 hours, and the share of household members with malaria. Of the six interactions that test whether the effect differs by dwelling type, only household bed-net use survives. The data support “the campaign worked among solid-house dwellers” more firmly than “it worked differently by dwelling type,” and the second claim is the one the policy argument needs.

State the denominator. The working paper describes the urban reduction of 6.2 cases per million as about 30 percent of the pre-campaign monthly rate. That rate, 20.89, is the mean across all facilities. Every rate in the facility analysis divides cases by the subdistrict’s whole population, so the comparison is legitimate as far as it goes. In the subdistrict-months behind the urban estimate, all facilities recorded 20.3 cases per million before the campaign, and 6.21 is 31 percent of that. Against the 9.5 cases recorded at urban facilities, the outcome the regression actually models, the same point estimate is a 66 percent decline. The 2026 abstract gives the decline as 33 percent. The two readings answer different questions, and neither can be interpreted unless its base is stated next to it.

Show how much the sample rule matters. The administrative analysis keeps only subdistricts with at least half their population inside the study circles, to limit contamination of the controls; outside the circles, control districts did receive ads. At thresholds of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 percent, the urban estimate keeps its sign (−6.64, −4.82, −4.00, −5.79, −6.21). Under the paper’s cluster-robust test it is significant at the 5 percent level at 10, 40, and 50 percent but not at 20 or 30 percent. The threshold also changes how many districts identify the effect, from 74 at 10 percent to 50 at 50 percent. A wild cluster bootstrap (Section 44.1.5), which holds its size better with few or unbalanced clusters, leaves only the 10 percent sample significant (Table 75.6). The paper reports this table in an appendix. When the headline depends on a sample rule, the table belongs in the main text.

Carry the uncertainty into the cost ratio. The $6.54 figure divides by an overall effect whose 95 percent interval runs from about −16 to +10 cases per million. The ratio is finite only at the favorable end of that interval and unbounded at the other. The $3.35 figure divides by the urban estimate without controls, whose interval also includes zero (p = 0.08). Because that estimate counts urban-facility cases per resident, multiplying it by the whole population of the 22 states does not double-count. It does assume that every subdistrict has urban facilities like the 62 behind the estimate, which hold 69 percent of the sample’s population; restricting the effect to that share raises the figure to about $4.80. With a wild cluster bootstrap, every cost ratio in the paper has a best case between $1.22 and $1.58 and no finite worst case (Table 75.6). Report cost-effectiveness as an interval built from the interval of the effect, and say which population the effect applies to.

75.7.2 Power when the outcome is a rare disease

Measuring advertising effects is hard even when the outcome is sales, because the effects are small relative to the noise (Lewis and Rao 2015). It is harder when the outcome is a disease recorded at about 21 cases per million people per month and the facility estimates rest on only 56 of the 79 randomized districts, the ones with a subdistrict mostly inside the study circles (50 for the urban outcome). The chunk below asks what this design could detect, using the standard errors and means the working paper reports and the district counts from the replication package.

Code
# Design analysis of the administrative-data arm of Donati et al., using numbers
# printed in the 2024 working paper (Table 5, fully controlled columns (3), (6),
# (9), and the Table D7 means for the benchmark sample of 119 subdistricts) and
# the number of districts behind each estimate, counted in the replication
# package (see the replication subsection below).
z <- qnorm(0.975) + qnorm(0.80)        # 80% power, 5% two-sided test

adm <- data.frame(
  outcome  = c("Overall", "Urban facilities", "Rural facilities"),
  estimate = c(-4.68, -6.21, -2.24),   # cases per million people per month
  se       = c( 5.37,  2.86,  5.79),   # clustered by district
  mean     = c(20.89,  9.45, 18.87),   # subsample means, Table D7
  J        = c(56, 50, 56)             # districts (clusters) behind each estimate
)
adm$mde      <- z * adm$se
adm$mde_pct  <- 100 * adm$mde / adm$mean
adm$power_20 <- with(adm, pnorm(0.2 * mean / se - qnorm(0.975)) +
                          pnorm(-0.2 * mean / se - qnorm(0.975)))
# Districts needed for an MDE of 20% of the mean, scaling the SE by
# 1/sqrt(J) with subdistricts per district and panel length held fixed.
adm$districts_for_20pct <- ceiling(adm$J * (adm$mde / (0.2 * adm$mean))^2)

# Type M (exaggeration) ratio if the true urban effect were half the
# estimate: the average |estimate| among significant results, over |truth|.
type_m <- function(true, se, crit = qnorm(0.975)) {
  k <- crit * se
  p_hi <- 1 - pnorm((k - true) / se)
  p_lo <- pnorm((-k - true) / se)
  e_hi <- true * p_hi + se * dnorm((k - true) / se)
  e_lo <- true * p_lo - se * dnorm((-k - true) / se)
  c(power = p_hi + p_lo,
    exaggeration = ((e_hi - e_lo) / (p_hi + p_lo)) / abs(true))
}

For overall incidence, the design’s minimum detectable effect was about 15 cases per million, or 72 percent of the mean. Its chance of detecting a 20 percent reduction was about one in eight. Bringing the minimum detectable effect down to 20 percent would take roughly 730 districts, against the 126 in the three study states. That scaling holds the number of subdistricts per district and the length of the panel fixed, so treat it as a rough guide rather than a design.

The urban estimate, significant only under the conventional test, has a second problem. Suppose the true effect were half the estimate. The design would detect it about one time in five, and the estimates that did reach significance would overstate it by a factor of about 2.3 on average. Gelman and Carlin (2014) call this kind of exaggeration a Type M error. None of this makes the urban result wrong. It means the administrative arm can corroborate the survey evidence but cannot carry the claim on its own. The parts of the paper that were adequately powered are the individual-level survey outcomes, with thousands of respondents, and the mechanism experiment, whose outcome (bed-net use) is common.

When you design a study in this mould, run this calculation for the administrative outcome before launch. Then decide in advance which layer is the primary test, and write the abstract to match that decision.

Read against Section 75.4, the paper completes two legs of the analysis step that most program evaluations skip. Its comparison of cost per case with net distribution and vaccine campaigns prices an alternative initiative. Its central finding is an equity result in the trade-off sense: the benefits went to the group with less at stake. Read against Table 75.1, it shows what makes a theory-light evaluation a Marketing Science paper rather than a public-health one. The outcome is malaria, but the contribution is about delivery, targeting, and measurement beyond a platform’s own metrics. That contribution applies to any advertiser whose objective differs from the one the platform optimizes.

75.7.3 Replicating the campaign evaluation

The World Bank’s reproducibility package for the working paper holds the de-identified survey responses, the raw facility records (one spreadsheet per district and month, 1,778 in all), and the authors’ Stata code. The cleaning and estimation code was ported to R without running any of the package’s own code. The port reproduces every coefficient, standard error, and sample size in Tables 2 to 5, 7, and D17 to the printed precision, 60 of 60 estimates. Table 75.4 shows the ones this section quotes.

Two details of the port are worth knowing before you attempt one like it. The first is that the facility-data cleaning deletes every period from every spreadsheet cell before it converts text to numbers. Stata writes a whole-number cell as 1262, so nothing breaks. A reader that writes 1262.0 multiplies every count by ten, and every Table 5 estimate with it, so the code is correct only because of how one program prints numbers. The second is that Stata treats a missing value as larger than any number, so the rule that drops household malaria shares above one also drops missing shares. In the cross-section, that rule is what removes the junk answers. The survey files also carry Messenger user IDs and free-text answers. For that reason the replication script reads only the analysis columns and replaces the user ID with an integer index as soon as it loads. That is the minimum to expect of any script that handles chatbot-survey data.

The replication also yields a count the paper does not report. The facility analysis keeps subdistricts with more than half their population inside the study circles. Those 119 subdistricts lie in only 56 of the 79 randomized districts, and the urban estimate rests on 62 subdistricts in 50 districts. The power calculation in Section 75.7.2 uses these counts.

Table 75.5 answers the referee’s multiple-testing question for the six survey outcomes in Tables 2 to 4. It treats two families separately: the effects among solid-house dwellers, and the interactions that test whether the effect differs by dwelling type. The adjustment is the Romano–Wolf step-down, the procedure that List, Shaikh, and Xu (2019) build on for experiments with several outcomes and subgroups. Each step compares a t-statistic with the bootstrap distribution of the largest t-statistic among the hypotheses not yet rejected. The bootstrap draws one wild-cluster weight per district and applies it in all twelve regressions, so it keeps the correlation between outcomes reported by overlapping respondents.

Three of the six solid-house effects survive at the 5 percent level: household bed-net use (adjusted p = 0.013), help-seeking within 24 hours (0.024), and the share of household members with malaria (0.038). The respondent’s own bed-net use and the any-malaria indicator do not (0.14 each). Among the interactions, only household bed-net use survives (0.009). Holm’s simpler adjustment of the conventional p-values reaches the same verdicts. The evidence that the campaign changed behavior and health among solid-house dwellers is fairly strong. The evidence that its effect differed by dwelling type rests mainly on one outcome.

# Wild cluster bootstrap (Rademacher), one weight per district shared by every

wild_t <- function(m, v) {               # observed t and B bootstrap t's (WCU, CR0 scale)
  Xm <- model.matrix(m); y <- drop(model.matrix(m, type = "lhs")); w <- weights(m)
  if (is.null(w)) w <- rep(1, length(y))
  A <- solve(crossprod(Xm, Xm * w), t(Xm * w)); k <- match(v, colnames(Xm))
  stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(drop(A %*% y), unname(coef(m)), check.attributes = FALSE)))
  u <- drop(y - Xm %*% (A %*% y)); g <- m$g
  t0 <- sum(A[k, ] * y) / sqrt(sum(rowsum(A[k, ] * u, g)^2))
  tb <- numeric(B)
  for (ch in split(seq_len(B), ceiling(seq_len(B) / 1000))) {
    U <- u * Vg[g, ch, drop = FALSE]; Bs <- A %*% U; E <- U - Xm %*% Bs
    tb[ch] <- Bs[k, ] / sqrt(colSums(rowsum(A[k, ] * E, g)^2))
  }
  list(t = t0, tb = tb)
}
romano_wolf <- function(tl) {
  t0 <- abs(sapply(tl, `[[`, "t")); Tb <- abs(sapply(tl, `[[`, "tb"))
  o <- order(t0, decreasing = TRUE); p <- numeric(length(t0))
  for (s in seq_along(o)) {
    mxb <- apply(Tb[, o[s:length(o)], drop = FALSE], 1, max)
    p[o[s]] <- max(mean(mxb >= t0[o[s]]), if (s > 1) p[o[s - 1]] else 0)
  }
  p
}

# ---- Extension 3: wild cluster bootstrap (WCR) for the HMIS estimates ------------------
# Null imposed, Rademacher weights by district, fixed effects projected out first.
# The interval inverts the bootstrap test; every bootstrap quantity is linear in the
# null value, so the inversion is exact for the given draws.
wcr <- function(m, v = "treatment_post", level = 0.95) {
  Xm <- model.matrix(m, type = "rhs"); y <- drop(model.matrix(m, type = "lhs"))
  Xm <- Xm[, colnames(Xm) %in% names(coef(m)), drop = FALSE]
  dd <- demean(cbind(y, Xm), as.data.frame(m$fixef_id)); yd <- dd[, 1]; Xd <- dd[, -1, drop = FALSE]
  k <- match(v, colnames(Xd)); qz <- qr(Xd[, -k, drop = FALSE]); qx <- qr(Xd)
  xt <- qr.resid(qz, Xd[, k]); ry <- qr.resid(qz, yd); ci <- xt / sum(xt^2)
  b <- sum(ci * yd); stopifnot(abs(b - coef(m)[v]) < 1e-8)
  g <- m$g; G <- sort(unique(g)); se0 <- sqrt(sum(rowsum(ci * qr.resid(qx, yd), g)^2))
  set.seed(20240902)
  Vd <- matrix(sample(c(-1, 1), length(G) * B, TRUE), length(G), B)
  sP <- sQ <- matrix(0, length(G), B); nP <- nQ <- numeric(B)
  for (ch in split(seq_len(B), ceiling(seq_len(B) / 1000))) {
    V <- Vd[match(g, G), ch, drop = FALSE]
    sP[, ch] <- rowsum(ci * qr.resid(qx, ry * V), g); sQ[, ch] <- rowsum(ci * qr.resid(qx, xt * V), g)
    nP[ch] <- colSums(ci * ry * V); nQ[ch] <- colSums(ci * xt * V)
  }
  pv <- function(b0) mean(abs((nP - b0 * nQ) / sqrt(colSums((sP - b0 * sQ)^2))) >= abs((b - b0) / se0))
  bound <- function(out, inn) { for (i in 1:50) { mid <- (out + inn) / 2
    if (pv(mid) > 1 - level) inn <- mid else out <- mid }; (out + inn) / 2 }
  c(b = b, se = unname(se(m)[v]), G = length(G), p_cluster = unname(pvalue(m)[v]), p_wcr = pv(0),
    lo_t = unname(confint(m, v, level = level)[[1]]), hi_t = unname(confint(m, v, level = level)[[2]]),
    lo_wcr = bound(b - 30 * se0, b), hi_wcr = bound(b + 30 * se0, b))
}

75.9 Writing Craft

A few conventions distinguish papers of this type that read well from those that read defensively.

Concede the intended effect first. The rule worked on its own terms; say so in the abstract. Papers that suppress the intended effect to dramatize the unintended one read as advocacy.

Label the epistemic status of every claim. “We estimate,” “model-based evidence implies,” and “this is suggestive” are three different commitments. Using the strongest available verb for the weakest available evidence is the fastest way to lose a referee.

Put the distributional result in the abstract. The average effect is rarely the interesting one in a policy paper, and the subgroup pattern is usually what a policy audience acts on. “Eight percent on average, fifteen percent among the less affluent” is a better sentence than either number alone.

Give the reader one number to remember. In the anchor paper it is the break-even shock probability implied by the rate ratio. A policy paper that leaves the reader with a rule of thumb travels further than one that leaves them with a table.

Write the model section for someone who will skip it. State the result of the model in prose immediately before the algebra, so a behavioral reader gets the threshold intuition and a quantitative reader gets the derivation.

The general craft conventions—framing a contribution, structuring an introduction, handling the review process—are treated at length in Chapter 76 and Chapter 77, and the reporting standards a multi-method paper must meet are in Chapter 78.

  • Most modern consumer regulation restricts a marketing instrument, which makes the substitution, effectiveness, and reachability questions it raises marketing questions with a marketing comparative advantage.
  • The same study is a different paper at each outlet (Table 75.1); choose the target before the analysis is finished, because the section order, figure set, and welfare object all follow from it.
  • The five-move template (Figure 75.1)—shock, spillover, normative model, mechanism, welfare plus corroboration—is ordered so that each move answers the objection raised by the previous one.
  • The template generalizes to initiatives with no posted price ratio through the analysis–assessment scaffold (Kaul, Luo, and Singh 2026): describe goal realization, spillovers, and performance relative to alternative ways of organizing, then assess by Pareto improvement, a single net-impact metric, or an explicit accounting of trade-offs in magnitude, equity, certainty, and agency (Figure 75.2). The alternative-initiative leg is the opportunity-cost comparison marketing is best equipped to price, and agency is the trade-off dimension the field knows best.
  • Translate the vocabulary deliberately (Table 75.2); it is what connects the paper to marketing literatures instead of importing an economics debate.
  • Screen candidate projects on five conditions, of which the binding ones are observing the same units in both markets and having exposure variation independent of the policy date.
  • Power for spillover designs is a real constraint: second-order effects on sticky outcomes need large panels, and the design should be sized before data collection.
  • Every effective response in the objection playbook (Table 75.7) is something built into the design in advance; none can be manufactured during a revision.
  • In the writing, concede the intended effect first, label the epistemic status of each claim, put the distributional result in the abstract, and leave the reader with one decision-relevant number.
  • A second, theory-light paper type is the randomized evaluation of a real campaign (Donati et al. 2026a), and it wins on design (Section 75.7). Randomize the actual program at the cluster where it is delivered. Measure off-platform outcomes in layers. Power the heterogeneity on a risk marker fixed in advance. Separate content from reach with a second experiment, and price the result against alternative programs. Rare administrative outcomes are seldom powered at feasible cluster counts (Section 75.7.2), so they corroborate the claim rather than carry it.

# Writing the Policy-and-Welfare Paper for Marketing Journals {#sec-policy-papers-marketing}

The preceding three chapters assembled a research architecture: a causal estimate
of a spillover into an untargeted market, a normative model that says what
optimal behavior looks like, a calibrated welfare bound, and an independent
complementary test. The architecture is field-neutral. Whether a paper built on it
lands in *Management Science*, *Marketing Science*, the *Journal of Marketing*, the
*Journal of Marketing Research*, the *Journal of Consumer Research*, or the
*Journal of Public Policy & Marketing* depends on choices about framing, about
which component carries the contribution, and about whose decision problem the
paper is written for.

This chapter is about those choices. It maps the architecture onto the marketing
journals, sets out the five-move template that these papers follow, translates
"consumer financial protection" into the marketing-instrument vocabulary that
makes the question ours rather than borrowed, supplies a portfolio of concrete
project designs, and closes with the objections reviewers raise and how to
pre-empt them.

## Why This Paper Type Belongs in Marketing {#sec-pwm-belongs}

There is a reflex to treat consumer-protection research as economics that happens
to involve consumers. The reflex is wrong for a specific reason: **almost every
consequential consumer regulation of the past two decades restricts a marketing
instrument.** Section 304 of the CARD Act did not cap interest rates or limit
credit lines; it banned marketing—the tables, the giveaways, the on-campus
promotion of a financial product to a young audience [@brown2026spillover].
Advertising bans restrict advertising [@dubois2018banning]. Privacy regimes
restrict targeting and data joins [@goldfarb2011privacy; @johnson2023privacy;
@aridor2023privacy]. Do-not-call and anti-spam rules restrict channels
[@goh2011newspaper]. Disclosure mandates restrict message content. Dark-pattern
rules restrict interface design.

Once a rule is understood as a constraint on the marketing mix, the questions it
raises are marketing questions. What is the cross-elasticity between the
restricted instrument and its substitutes? Which segments were reachable only
through the restricted instrument? How does the firm reallocate budget, and what
does the reallocation do to the consumers who were the rule's intended
beneficiaries? Marketing owns the substitution structure of consumer choice, the
measurement of instrument effectiveness, and the models of how firms allocate
across instruments. That is a comparative advantage, and it is the argument the
introduction of such a paper should make in its second paragraph.

The field's editorial statements have been explicit that work bearing on consumer
and societal welfare is wanted, not tolerated [@chandy2021better;
@moorman2019challenging], and the transformative-consumer-research tradition
supplies a well-developed vocabulary for well-being outcomes [@mick2012tcr;
@wilkie1999contributions]. What has been scarcer is work that combines that
orientation with the identification and modeling standards of the quantitative
track. The architecture in these chapters is one way to supply it.

## The Journal-Fit Map {#sec-pwm-fit}

The same underlying study can be written five ways. @tbl-pwm-fit sets out what
each outlet wants as the load-bearing contribution, what role the model plays, and
what the review process will attack hardest.

|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *Marketing Science* / *QME* | Identification plus model; a new estimand or a novel identification of substitution | Central; often estimated rather than only calibrated | Consumer surplus, sometimes total surplus | "Why not estimate the model?"; instrument validity |
| *Management Science* | A general economic mechanism demonstrated in a consequential setting | Central but may be a bounding device | Consumer welfare with explicit assumptions | Generality of the mechanism; robustness of the bound |
| *Journal of Marketing Research* | A clean causal fact plus measurement innovation | Supporting; disciplines the hypotheses | Behavioral outcome, welfare secondary | Design threats; external validity |
| *Journal of Marketing* | Managerial and societal implication; framework contribution | Framing device, kept light | Firm outcomes plus consumer well-being | "So what for managers and policymakers?" |
| *Journal of Consumer Research* | Psychological process; the consumer's experience of the constraint | Usually replaced by a process theory | Consumer well-being, subjectively measured | Process evidence; alternative accounts |
| *Journal of Public Policy & Marketing* | Direct policy relevance and prescription | Supporting; the policy question leads | Consumer welfare and distributional equity | Depth of institutional detail; prescriptive clarity |

: How the same policy-and-welfare study is positioned across marketing outlets. The study does not change; the ordering of the moves and the identity of the contribution do. {#tbl-pwm-fit}

Two implications are worth stating plainly. First, the *Marketing Science* version
and the *Journal of Marketing* version are not the same paper with different
abstracts—they have different section orders, different figure sets, and different
robustness sections. Deciding the outlet after the analysis is finished is a
common and expensive mistake. Second, the welfare object is a positioning choice,
not merely a technical one: a paper whose welfare object is firm profit is a
strategy paper, and one whose object is consumer surplus is a policy paper, and
reviewers evaluate them against different literatures.

## The Five-Move Template {#sec-pwm-template}

Papers with this architecture follow a recognizable sequence, shown in
@fig-pwm-moves. Each move answers the objection raised by the previous one, which
is why the order is difficult to rearrange.

```{mermaid}
%%| label: fig-pwm-moves
%%| fig-cap: "The five-move architecture. Each move exists to answer the question the previous move provokes, which is why reordering them weakens the argument even when every component is unchanged."
flowchart TB
  M1["Move 1: a policy or platform change<br/>restricts one marketing instrument"] --> Q1{"Did behavior<br/>simply stop?"}
  Q1 --> M2["Move 2: document the spillover<br/>into the untargeted market"]
  M2 --> Q2{"Was the substitution<br/>good or bad for the consumer?"}
  Q2 --> M3["Move 3: normative model<br/>defines optimal behavior"]
  M3 --> Q3{"Do consumers actually<br/>behave that way?"}
  Q3 --> M4["Move 4: mechanism evidence<br/>measures the departures"]
  M4 --> Q4{"Is the welfare claim only<br/>as good as the model?"}
  Q4 --> M5["Move 5: complementary analysis<br/>on downstream real outcomes"]
  M5 --> C["Contribution: a signed, bounded<br/>welfare conclusion with<br/>distributional detail"]
```

**Move 1 — the institutional shock.** Establish that a specific instrument became
more costly on a specific date, with enough institutional detail that a reader can
see who was exposed and who was not. This section is longer than authors expect
and is where the identification is actually won.

**Move 2 — the spillover.** Estimate the effect on the untargeted market with a
design whose control group is defined by exposure rather than geography
(@sec-policy-spillovers). Lead with the event study, not the pooled coefficient.
Include the theory-predicted heterogeneity as a specification test.

**Move 3 — the normative model.** Write down the small model, derive the threshold,
and state which departures are possible (@sec-normative-benchmarks). Two to four
pages. Resist estimating it.

**Move 4 — mechanism.** Measure which departures are present and in what
proportion, ideally with a record-linked survey (@sec-complementary-evidence).
This section supplies the population shares the welfare calculation needs, so it
must come before the welfare section.

**Move 5 — welfare and corroboration.** Calibrate the bound
(@sec-welfare-calibration), report it by subgroup with a sensitivity analysis, and
then present an independent downstream test whose failure modes differ.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Anatomy of the abstract

The anchor paper's abstract is a compact instance of the template and repays
imitation. It names the provision and what it did; concedes that the intended
effect occurred ("while it reduced card use"); states the spillover with a
magnitude and a distributional split; explains that a survey was designed to
assess the substitution and what it revealed; states that model-based evidence
implies a welfare gain; and closes with the complementary analysis of academic
outcomes. Six sentences, five moves, one concession. The concession is doing
strategic work: acknowledging the intended effect first buys the credibility to
report the unintended one.
:::

## Generalizing the Template: Analysis and Assessment {#sec-pwm-kaul}

The five moves above are a template for one paper type: a regulatory restriction with
an untargeted substitute market and a price ratio to anchor a normative cutoff. Most
initiatives a marketing scholar would want to evaluate are not shaped that way. A
brand's supplier-diversity commitment, a retailer's bag charge, a platform's safety
re-ranking, a firm's decision to fund a community program instead of cutting prices—
each has social consequences and none has a posted price ratio. @kaul2026social supply
the more general scaffold, and it is worth reading alongside the template because it
shows which of the five moves is doing general work and which is an artifact of the
anchor paper's setting.

Their starting definition is the one @sec-causal-potential-outcomes already uses,
relocated from a treatment to an organizational initiative: **impact is the difference
between the states of the world with and without the initiative**. Everything follows
from taking that counterfactual seriously. The framework then splits into a descriptive
step and a normative one, and the split is the source of most of its discipline,
because the two steps fail in different ways and are usually conflated.

The **analysis step** is descriptive and has three legs. *Goal realization* asks how far
the initiative achieved the social goal it stated—the question a program evaluation
usually stops at. *Spillovers* ask what else it moved, financially or societally, which
is the same object @sec-policy-spillovers is built around, arrived at from a different
literature and given the same name. *Alternative initiatives* ask how the initiative
performed relative to other ways of organizing to achieve the same goal.

That third leg is the one the five-move template does not have, and it is the more
demanding bar. Move 2 asks whether behavior stopped or relocated; it compares the
policy to no policy. Kaul and coauthors ask what else the organization could have done
with the same resources, which is an opportunity-cost counterfactual. A finding that a
loyalty-funded literacy program raised literacy is a Move-2 finding. A finding that it
raised literacy *less per dollar than a price cut to the same households would have*
is an analysis-step finding, and it is the one a manager can act on. Marketing is
unusually well placed to supply it, because a firm's alternative uses of the same
budget are typically marketing instruments whose effects the field already knows how
to estimate.

The **assessment step** is normative, and its contribution is to name three routes
rather than one. *Pareto improvement* asks whether anyone was made worse off—the
cleanest standard, and rarely satisfied, since almost every marketing intervention
takes from someone. *Net impact* aggregates benefits and costs into a single metric,
which is what the welfare bound of @sec-welfare-calibration does; it buys comparability
by committing to an exchange rate between outcomes, and that commitment is the
assumption reviewers should attack. *Trade-off evaluation* declines the exchange rate
and characterizes the trade-offs instead, along four dimensions: relative **magnitude**,
**equity** (who bears the cost), **certainty** (how confidently each side is known), and
**agency** (whether those affected chose the exposure).

that distinction has thrown away the variable the field knows most about.

## Writing the paper in this mould

The framework is a research agenda, and the agenda is written for a management
audience; the marketing version is not yet written. A tractable project takes one
initiative and does the analysis step *completely*, which almost nobody does.

1. **Name the initiative and its counterfactual explicitly**, in the with-and-without
   form. Most papers name the initiative and leave the counterfactual implicit, which
   is where the disagreements later turn out to live.
2. **Estimate goal realization with the field's own designs.** This is a standard
   causal-inference exercise (@sec-causal-inference) and should be the shortest part.
3. **Search for spillovers where theory says substitution goes**, using the taxonomy in
   @sec-psp-taxonomy. Report the untargeted-market effect with the same care as the
   targeted one.
4. **Construct at least one alternative initiative and price it.** The credible ones
   are the firm's own foregone options: the same budget spent on price, on the
   salesforce, on the product. This is the move that makes it a marketing paper rather
   than a program evaluation.
5. **Choose the assessment route in the paper, and defend the choice.** Test Pareto
   first because it is cheap and occasionally passes. If it fails, either commit to a
   metric and defend the exchange rate, or report the four trade-off dimensions and
   decline to aggregate. Declining to aggregate is a legitimate result, provided the
   dimensions are measured rather than gestured at.
6. **Triangulate the normative claim** the way @sec-complementary-evidence prescribes,
   since a conclusion that survives two assessment routes is worth far more than one
   that survives a longer robustness table under a single route.

The reviewer objection to anticipate is that the alternative-initiative comparison is
unidentified, because the firm did not run the alternative. It is a fair objection and
the answer is a bound: pick alternatives whose effects the literature has already
pinned down with meta-analytic precision—advertising elasticities, price elasticities,
salesforce response—and report the comparison as an interval. A bounded opportunity
cost is more informative than a precisely estimated goal-realization effect with no
comparison at all.
:::

Row 10 has a precedent outside the policy literature. @kim2026wageaccess study
on-demand wage access, an employer-linked benefit that lets low-wage workers draw
earned pay before the pay date, as an alternative to costly short-term credit such
as payday loans. Using transaction records from about 4,000 users of one U.S.
platform and a staggered-adoption difference-in-differences design, they find that
adoption raises monthly saving frequency by 3.7 percent and time spent monitoring
financial dashboards by 12.9 percent, with larger effects where minimum wages are
lower or banking access is thinner. The design observes the same units before and
after the new instrument arrives, which is the binding constraint named above. It
does not observe the credit products the platform may displace, so the substitution
leg of the architecture remains open.

::: {.callout-note}
## Screening candidate projects before you invest

A project is viable only if all five of the following hold. (1) The shock has a
date and a scope you can document from primary sources. (2) The substitute market
is observed for the *same* units. (3) There is exposure variation independent of
the shock date. (4) The two instruments have posted or recoverable prices, since
the normative cutoff is a price ratio. (5) A downstream outcome exists that theory
says should move. Failing (2) or (3) kills the project; failing (4) downgrades it
from a welfare paper to a substitution paper, which is still publishable but in a
different place.
:::

### Sizing the design before collecting data {#sec-pwm-power}

Spillover effects are second-order by construction—a fraction of an already
modest first-order effect—so power is a real constraint rather than a formality.
The calculation below gives the minimum detectable effect for a two-group panel
design of the kind used throughout these chapters, as a function of the number of
units and the within-unit correlation of the outcome.

```{r pwm-mde}
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false
#| label: fig-pwm-mde
#| fig-cap: "Minimum detectable effect for a two-group spillover panel, at 80 percent power and a 5 percent two-sided test, as a share of the outcome's standard deviation. Repeated observations per unit help, but with diminishing returns that arrive quickly once the within-unit correlation is high, which is typical of balances, spend, and other sticky consumer outcomes."

library(ggplot2)

mde <- function(n_units, n_periods, rho = 0.6, p_treat = 0.5,
                power = 0.80, alpha = 0.05) {
  # Design effect for repeated measures on the same unit.
  deff <- 1 + (n_periods - 1) * rho
  n_eff <- n_units * n_periods / deff
  (qnorm(1 - alpha / 2) + qnorm(power)) *
    sqrt(1 / (n_eff * p_treat * (1 - p_treat)))
}

pw <- expand.grid(n_units = c(2000, 5000, 20000, 70000),
                  n_periods = 2:12, rho = c(0.3, 0.7))
pw$mde <- mapply(mde, pw$n_units, pw$n_periods, pw$rho)

ggplot(pw, aes(n_periods, mde, colour = factor(n_units))) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 0.8) +
  facet_wrap(~ paste("Within-unit correlation =", rho)) +
  scale_colour_discrete(name = "Units") +
  labs(x = "Periods observed per unit",
       y = "Minimum detectable effect (SD units)",
       title = "Power for a spillover panel design") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 11)
```

The practical reading: with the roughly 70,000 students and eight semesters of the
anchor study, effects well under a tenth of a standard deviation are detectable,
which is why an 8 percent movement in balances could be estimated precisely enough
to support subgroup analysis. A study with two thousand units and three periods is
not going to find a spillover unless it is enormous, and should be redesigned
rather than run.

## A Second Paper Type: The Randomized Campaign Evaluation {#sec-pwm-campaign-eval}

The architecture of the preceding chapters is one way to write a policy paper.
There is a simpler type that uses almost no theory. It takes a marketing program
that someone actually ran, randomizes it at scale, and measures the outcomes the
program exists to change. It belongs in this chapter because its contribution is
still a policy contribution. It is also the type a doctoral student can most
realistically execute, because it needs a partner and a design rather than a model.
@donati2026malaria, published in *Marketing Science*, is a clean recent instance.
Their question is the title of the paper: can Facebook ads prevent malaria? They
answer it with two field experiments, administrative health records, and a
cost-per-case calculation.

::: {.callout-note}
## Which version the numbers come from

The figures in this section come from the 2024 World Bank working paper
[@donati2024malariawp]. Its tables are open, and the World Bank's reproducibility
package for it holds the data and the authors' code. Every estimate quoted here
from Tables 2 to 5, 7, and D17 was reproduced from that package
(@sec-pwm-campaign-replication). Only a one-line teaser abstract of the
published article was accessible when this section was written. The 2026 CESifo
version [@donati2026malariacesifo] frames some results differently; those
differences are noted where they matter. Check the published tables before quoting
a number.
:::

The program was a social-marketing campaign by the nonprofit Malaria No More,
*Bite Ko Mat Lo Lite* ("don't take the bite lightly"), funded by Facebook's
Campaigns for a Healthier World initiative. Its ads urged people to sleep under bed
nets and to get tested within 24 hours of a fever. It ran on Facebook and Instagram
from July 2020 to early January 2021 and spent \$202,000. It reached about 130
million users in 22 states, at an average of 3.6 impressions each. It was optimized
for engagement: the team switched off weaker ads and moved budget to the
best-performing ones. Campaigns like this are usually judged by engagement and by
platform brand-lift surveys, and by those measures this one succeeded. The paper
asks two questions those measures cannot answer. Did the campaign change offline
behavior and disease? And did it work for the people most at risk?

The design has five moves, shown in @fig-pwm-campaign.

1. *Randomize the real campaign, not a copy of it.* The nonprofit agreed to delay
   launch in three high-burden states (Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, and Chhattisgarh)
   so that 40 of 80 districts could be randomized to receive it. One district was
   dropped because two districts shared a name on the randomization list, leaving
   79 for analysis. The platform cannot target ads by district boundary, so the
   team drew circles around each district's main cities and the advertiser excluded
   the control circles. Randomizing clusters rather than individuals matches how
   both the ads and the disease spread. It also makes every estimate an
   intent-to-treat effect of the campaign as it was actually delivered.
2. *Measure outcomes off the platform, in layers.* The team recruited respondents
   with ads that paid mobile credit for completing chatbot surveys on Messenger. A
   longitudinal sample of 5,205 reported behavior in up to eight short surveys
   during the campaign. A separately recruited sample of 3,052 reported household
   malaria one to three months after it ended. Monthly records of tests and
   positive cases by subdistrict, split into urban and rural facilities, came from
   India's Health Management Information System. Each layer covers a weakness of
   another. Surveys capture behavior but invite demand effects. Facility records
   cannot be talked into a result, but they are coarse and under-count cases.
3. *Fix a risk marker in advance and power both sides of it.* Formative surveys
   found that dwelling type was the strongest household predictor of malaria.
   Respondents in solid brick or cement houses were 43 percent less likely to
   report a recent case than those in mud, tin, or straw dwellings. Organic
   recruitment reached mostly solid-house dwellers, so recruitment was stratified
   by district and dwelling type. Lookalike audiences seeded with non-solid
   dwellers brought about 55 percent of respondents from higher-risk housing. The
   marker also maps onto the facility data, because non-solid dwellings make up
   roughly 15 percent of urban and 50 percent of rural housing in India.
4. *Separate content from reach.* A split by risk group cannot by itself say
   whether the ads failed to persuade the high-risk group or failed to reach it. A
   second experiment answers that question.
5. *Price the effect against alternatives.* Convert the estimate into a cost per
   case averted and set it beside other ways of spending the same money.

The headline result is a split. Among respondents in solid houses, the campaign
raised the share of survey waves in which they slept under a net by 5.5 percentage
points (SE 2.5; control mean 60 percent). It raised help-seeking within 24 hours of
a fever by 10.7 points (SE 3.5, among the 301 who reported one). It also cut
self-reported household malaria after the campaign by 2.4 points (SE 1.1) on a
control rate of 5.5 percent. Among respondents in non-solid houses, each of these
estimates is small and insignificant. The facility records point the same way.
Malaria cases recorded at urban facilities fell by 6.21 a month per million
residents (SE 2.86), while cases at rural facilities did not detectably fall
(−2.24, SE 5.79), and the overall estimate (−4.68, SE 5.37) cannot be
distinguished from zero. The urban estimate is significant under the paper's
conventional test but not under a wild cluster bootstrap (p = 0.09;
@sec-pwm-campaign-replication).

Was the problem content or reach? Ad recall at the end of the campaign rose 4.9
points among treated solid-house respondents but only 1.1 points (insignificant)
among non-solid ones, which suggests the ads never arrived. The second experiment
tested this directly. For two weeks, the team retargeted the campaign's best ads,
through a Custom Audience, to a random half of a fresh survey pool. They reached
about 90 percent of that half, at 4.9 impressions each. With exposure equalized,
bed-net use the previous night rose 7.5 points (SE 2.9) on a control rate of 65
percent. The rise was 7.6 points among solid dwellers and 8.1 among non-solid
dwellers, and the estimated interaction was exactly zero (SE 5.7). So the ads
worked for the high-risk group once they reached it. The engagement-optimized
campaign mostly did not reach it, and the recruitment data show why in prices:
recruiting a respondent in a non-solid house cost almost ten times as much.

That finding is the paper's marketing contribution. Suppose a delivery algorithm is
told to maximize engagement within a budget. It will spend on the cheapest
responsive users, the mechanism @lambrecht2019algorithmic documented for job ads. In
public health, the cheapest users are often the ones who need the message least.
The cost calculation then completes the template. It multiplies the monthly effect
by nine months and by the 1.1 billion people in the campaign's states. That gives
\$3.35 per reported case averted using the urban estimate and \$6.54 using the
overall estimate. The paper sets these beside \$5–6 per case averted for distributing
insecticide-treated nets and \$5.68 per additional COVID-19 vaccination from a
meta-analysis of more than 800 Facebook public-health experiments
[@athey2023digital].

### Reading it as a referee would

A referee should look hard at four features of the paper. Each one is also a
lesson for anyone writing in this mould.

The heterogeneity carries the policy content, so it needs the strongest possible
status. In the 2024 version, the dwelling-type split leads the abstract. The 2026
CESifo abstract leads instead with small and insignificant average effects. It
reframes the split in terms of socioeconomic status and calls it a post-hoc
heterogeneity analysis. The 2024 working paper reports ethical approval but no
trial-registry identifier (the published article's front matter could not be
checked). Compare @breza2021facebook, who randomized U.S. counties
to a Facebook campaign of doctors' and nurses' videos before the 2020 holidays.
They list their trial registrations in the abstract, name three primary outcomes,
and report each one: less holiday travel, no change in the share of people leaving
home, and 3.5 percent fewer recorded infections. The malaria team chose its risk
marker in formative research before the trial and stratified on it, so the subgroup
analysis was built into the design; a registration would have let the paper say so.
With many outcomes and two strata, a referee will also ask for a multiple-testing
adjustment [@list2019multiple]. The replication package makes one possible
(@tbl-pwm-malaria-mt). Across the six survey outcomes, three effects among
solid-house dwellers survive at the 5 percent level: household bed-net use,
help-seeking within 24 hours, and the share of household members with malaria. Of
the six interactions that test whether the effect *differs* by dwelling type, only
household bed-net use survives. The data support "the campaign worked among
solid-house dwellers" more firmly than "it worked differently by dwelling type,"
and the second claim is the one the policy argument needs.

State the denominator. The working paper describes the urban reduction of 6.2 cases
per million as about 30 percent of the pre-campaign monthly rate. That rate, 20.89,
is the mean across all facilities. Every rate in the facility analysis divides
cases by the subdistrict's whole population, so the comparison is legitimate as
far as it goes. In the subdistrict-months behind the urban estimate, all facilities
recorded 20.3 cases per million before the campaign, and 6.21 is 31 percent of
that. Against the 9.5 cases recorded at urban facilities, the outcome the
regression actually models, the same point estimate is a 66 percent decline. The
2026 abstract gives the decline as 33 percent. The two readings answer different
questions, and neither can be interpreted unless its base is stated next to it.

Show how much the sample rule matters. The administrative analysis keeps only
subdistricts with at least half their population inside the study circles, to limit
contamination of the controls; outside the circles, control districts did receive
ads. At thresholds of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 percent, the urban estimate keeps its
sign (−6.64, −4.82, −4.00, −5.79, −6.21). Under the paper's cluster-robust test it
is significant at the 5 percent level at 10, 40, and 50 percent but not at 20 or 30
percent. The threshold also changes how many districts identify the effect, from 74
at 10 percent to 50 at 50 percent. A wild cluster bootstrap (@sec-causal-week05),
which holds its size better with few or unbalanced clusters, leaves only the 10
percent sample significant (@tbl-pwm-malaria-hmis). The paper reports this table in
an appendix. When the headline depends on a sample rule, the table belongs in the
main text.

Carry the uncertainty into the cost ratio. The \$6.54 figure divides by an overall
effect whose 95 percent interval runs from about −16 to +10 cases per million. The
ratio is finite only at the favorable end of that interval and unbounded at the
other. The \$3.35 figure divides by the urban estimate without controls, whose
interval also includes zero (p = 0.08). Because that estimate counts urban-facility
cases per resident, multiplying it by the whole population of the 22 states does
not double-count. It does assume that every subdistrict has urban facilities like
the 62 behind the estimate, which hold 69 percent of the sample's population;
restricting the effect to that share raises the figure to about \$4.80. With a wild
cluster bootstrap, every cost ratio in the paper has a best case between \$1.22 and
\$1.58 and no finite worst case (@tbl-pwm-malaria-hmis). Report cost-effectiveness
as an interval built from the interval of the effect, and say which population the
effect applies to.

### Power when the outcome is a rare disease {#sec-pwm-campaign-power}

Measuring advertising effects is hard even when the outcome is sales, because the
effects are small relative to the noise [@Lewis_2015]. It is harder when the outcome
is a disease recorded at about 21 cases per million people per month and the
facility estimates rest on only 56 of the 79 randomized districts, the ones with a
subdistrict mostly inside the study circles (50 for the urban outcome). The chunk
below asks what this design could detect, using the standard errors and means the
working paper reports and the district counts from the replication package.

```{r pwm-malaria-power}
#| echo: true
#| message: false
#| warning: false

# Design analysis of the administrative-data arm of Donati et al., using numbers
# printed in the 2024 working paper (Table 5, fully controlled columns (3), (6),
# (9), and the Table D7 means for the benchmark sample of 119 subdistricts) and
# the number of districts behind each estimate, counted in the replication
# package (see the replication subsection below).
z <- qnorm(0.975) + qnorm(0.80)        # 80% power, 5% two-sided test

adm <- data.frame(
  outcome  = c("Overall", "Urban facilities", "Rural facilities"),
  estimate = c(-4.68, -6.21, -2.24),   # cases per million people per month
  se       = c( 5.37,  2.86,  5.79),   # clustered by district
  mean     = c(20.89,  9.45, 18.87),   # subsample means, Table D7
  J        = c(56, 50, 56)             # districts (clusters) behind each estimate
)
adm$mde      <- z * adm$se
adm$mde_pct  <- 100 * adm$mde / adm$mean
adm$power_20 <- with(adm, pnorm(0.2 * mean / se - qnorm(0.975)) +
                          pnorm(-0.2 * mean / se - qnorm(0.975)))
# Districts needed for an MDE of 20% of the mean, scaling the SE by
# 1/sqrt(J) with subdistricts per district and panel length held fixed.
adm$districts_for_20pct <- ceiling(adm$J * (adm$mde / (0.2 * adm$mean))^2)
print(transform(adm, mde = round(mde, 1), mde_pct = round(mde_pct),
                power_20 = round(power_20, 2)), row.names = FALSE)

# Type M (exaggeration) ratio if the true urban effect were half the
# estimate: the average |estimate| among significant results, over |truth|.
type_m <- function(true, se, crit = qnorm(0.975)) {
  k <- crit * se
  p_hi <- 1 - pnorm((k - true) / se)
  p_lo <- pnorm((-k - true) / se)
  e_hi <- true * p_hi + se * dnorm((k - true) / se)
  e_lo <- true * p_lo - se * dnorm((-k - true) / se)
  c(power = p_hi + p_lo,
    exaggeration = ((e_hi - e_lo) / (p_hi + p_lo)) / abs(true))
}
round(type_m(true = -6.21 / 2, se = 2.86), 2)

# Cost per reported case averted, as in Section 7 of the working paper:
# monthly effect x 9 months x 1,100 million people; USD 202,000 ad spend.
cost_per_case <- function(effect) 202000 / (abs(effect) * 9 * 1100)
sprintf("%.3f", c(overall = cost_per_case(-3.12), urban = cost_per_case(-6.10)))

# The overall estimate's 95% interval (column 1: -3.12, SE 6.37) includes
# zero, so the ratio is finite only at the favourable end of the interval.
ci <- -3.12 + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(0.975) * 6.37
round(ci, 1)
sprintf("%.2f", cost_per_case(ci[1]))
```

For overall incidence, the design's minimum detectable effect was about 15 cases
per million, or 72 percent of the mean. Its chance of detecting a 20 percent
reduction was about one in eight. Bringing the minimum detectable effect down to 20
percent would take roughly 730 districts, against the 126 in the three study
states. That scaling holds the number of subdistricts per district and the length of
the panel fixed, so treat it as a rough guide rather than a design.

The urban estimate, significant only under the conventional test, has a second
problem. Suppose the true effect were
half the estimate. The design would detect it about one time in five, and the
estimates that did reach significance would overstate it by a factor of about 2.3
on average. @gelman2014beyond call this kind of exaggeration a Type M error. None of
this makes the urban result wrong. It means the administrative arm can corroborate
the survey evidence but cannot carry the claim on its own. The parts of the paper
that were adequately powered are the individual-level survey outcomes, with
thousands of respondents, and the mechanism experiment, whose outcome (bed-net use)
is common.

When you design a study in this mould, run this calculation for the administrative
outcome before launch. Then decide in advance which layer is the primary test, and
write the abstract to match that decision.

Read against @sec-pwm-kaul, the paper completes two legs of the analysis step that
most program evaluations skip. Its comparison of cost per case with net distribution
and vaccine campaigns prices an alternative initiative. Its central finding is an
equity result in the trade-off sense: the benefits went to the group with less at
stake. Read against @tbl-pwm-fit, it shows what makes a theory-light evaluation a
*Marketing Science* paper rather than a public-health one. The outcome is malaria,
but the contribution is about delivery, targeting, and measurement beyond a
platform's own metrics. That contribution applies to any advertiser whose objective
differs from the one the platform optimizes.

### Replicating the campaign evaluation {#sec-pwm-campaign-replication}

The World Bank's reproducibility package for the working paper holds the
de-identified survey responses, the raw facility records (one spreadsheet per
district and month, 1,778 in all), and the authors' Stata code. The cleaning and
estimation code was ported to R without running any of the package's own code.
The port reproduces every coefficient, standard error, and sample size in Tables 2
to 5, 7, and D17 to the printed precision, 60 of 60 estimates.
@tbl-pwm-malaria-repl shows the ones this section quotes.

Two details of the port are worth knowing before you attempt one like it. The
first is that the facility-data cleaning deletes every period from every
spreadsheet cell before it converts text to numbers. Stata writes a whole-number
cell as 1262, so nothing breaks. A reader that writes 1262.0 multiplies every
count by ten, and every Table 5 estimate with it, so the code is correct only
because of how one program prints numbers. The second is that Stata treats a
missing value as larger than any number, so the rule that drops household malaria
shares above one also drops missing shares. In the cross-section, that rule is
what removes the junk answers. The survey files also carry Messenger user IDs
and free-text answers. For that reason the replication script reads only the
analysis columns and replaces the user ID with an integer index as soon as it
loads. That is the minimum to expect of any script that handles chatbot-survey
data.

analysis keeps subdistricts with more than half their population inside the study

and subgroups. Each step compares a t-statistic with the bootstrap distribution of
the largest t-statistic among the hypotheses not yet rejected. The bootstrap draws
one wild-cluster weight per district and applies it in all twelve regressions, so
it keeps the correlation between outcomes reported by overlapping respondents.

evidence that its effect differed by dwelling type rests mainly on one outcome.

## The Reviewer Objection Playbook {#sec-pwm-objections}

@tbl-pwm-objections lists the objections this paper type reliably attracts, in
roughly the order they appear in reports, with the response that works and the
response that does not.

| Objection | Response that works | Response that fails |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "The effect is a confound from the concurrent macro shock" | An enumerated gauntlet pairing each channel with a test whose result would differ under the rival (@tbl-psp-gauntlet) | More controls; a longer robustness table of the same specification |
| "Why not estimate the structural model?" | Show the welfare conclusion holds for an entire preference class; state which functional forms theory cannot discipline | Estimating it anyway with an arbitrary form |
| "Your survey postdates the policy" | Name the assumption, test it against contemporaneous outside surveys, and show the direction of any bias | Asserting the sample is representative |
| "Welfare rests on your calibration" | Publish the ledger, the break-even value of the pivotal input, and the surface over the two quantities that matter | Reporting a single point estimate with a standard error |
| "This is a finance paper" | Frame as marketing-instrument regulation; connect to cross-instrument substitution and segment reachability (@tbl-pwm-translation) | Adding a paragraph on managerial implications at the end |
| "The complementary analysis looks harvested" | Pre-commit it, motivate it from the theory before estimating, and include a placebo outcome | Reporting five downstream outcomes and discussing the two that moved |
| "What should a manager or regulator do?" | A stated decision rule in the units of the decision: the break-even shock probability, the segment where the sign flips | A generic call for consumer education |
| "External validity: one institution, one product" | Argue the mechanism's scope conditions explicitly and show the price ratio and shock probability that would be needed elsewhere | Claiming the setting is representative |

: The objection playbook. Every "response that works" is something that must be built into the design in advance; none can be added during a revision. {#tbl-pwm-objections}

## Writing Craft {#sec-pwm-craft}

A few conventions distinguish papers of this type that read well from those that
read defensively.

**Concede the intended effect first.** The rule worked on its own terms; say so in
the abstract. Papers that suppress the intended effect to dramatize the unintended
one read as advocacy.

**Label the epistemic status of every claim.** "We estimate," "model-based evidence
implies," and "this is suggestive" are three different commitments. Using the
strongest available verb for the weakest available evidence is the fastest way to
lose a referee.

**Put the distributional result in the abstract.** The average effect is rarely the
interesting one in a policy paper, and the subgroup pattern is usually what a
policy audience acts on. "Eight percent on average, fifteen percent among the less
affluent" is a better sentence than either number alone.

**Give the reader one number to remember.** In the anchor paper it is the
break-even shock probability implied by the rate ratio. A policy paper that leaves
the reader with a rule of thumb travels further than one that leaves them with a
table.

**Write the model section for someone who will skip it.** State the result of the
model in prose immediately before the algebra, so a behavioral reader gets the
threshold intuition and a quantitative reader gets the derivation.

The general craft conventions—framing a contribution, structuring an introduction,
handling the review process—are treated at length in @sec-scientific-writing and
@sec-review-process, and the reporting standards a multi-method paper must meet are
in @sec-reporting.

## Key Takeaways {#sec-pwm-takeaways}

- Most modern consumer regulation restricts a *marketing instrument*, which makes
  the substitution, effectiveness, and reachability questions it raises marketing
  questions with a marketing comparative advantage.
- The same study is a different paper at each outlet (@tbl-pwm-fit); choose the
  target before the analysis is finished, because the section order, figure set,
  and welfare object all follow from it.
- The five-move template (@fig-pwm-moves)—shock, spillover, normative model,
  mechanism, welfare plus corroboration—is ordered so that each move answers the
  objection raised by the previous one.
- The template generalizes to initiatives with no posted price ratio through the
  analysis–assessment scaffold [@kaul2026social]: describe goal realization,
  spillovers, *and* performance relative to alternative ways of organizing, then
  assess by Pareto improvement, a single net-impact metric, or an explicit accounting
  of trade-offs in magnitude, equity, certainty, and agency (@fig-pwm-kaul). The
  alternative-initiative leg is the opportunity-cost comparison marketing is best
  equipped to price, and agency is the trade-off dimension the field knows best.
- Translate the vocabulary deliberately (@tbl-pwm-translation); it is what connects
  the paper to marketing literatures instead of importing an economics debate.
- Screen candidate projects on five conditions, of which the binding ones are
  observing the same units in both markets and having exposure variation
  independent of the policy date.
- Power for spillover designs is a real constraint: second-order effects on sticky
  outcomes need large panels, and the design should be sized before data collection.
- Every effective response in the objection playbook (@tbl-pwm-objections) is
  something built into the design in advance; none can be manufactured during a
  revision.
- In the writing, concede the intended effect first, label the epistemic status of
  each claim, put the distributional result in the abstract, and leave the reader
  with one decision-relevant number.
- A second, theory-light paper type is the randomized evaluation of a real campaign
  [@donati2026malaria], and it wins on design (@sec-pwm-campaign-eval). Randomize
  the actual program at the cluster where it is delivered. Measure off-platform
  outcomes in layers. Power the heterogeneity on a risk marker fixed in advance.
  Separate content from reach with a second experiment, and price the result against
  alternative programs. Rare administrative outcomes are seldom powered at feasible
  cluster counts (@sec-pwm-campaign-power), so they corroborate the claim rather
  than carry it.
